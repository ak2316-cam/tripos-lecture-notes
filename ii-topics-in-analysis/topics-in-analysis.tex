`Topics in Analysis' is a course with no real destination. It is a tour of some of the prettier shells lying on the beach of real and complex analysis, picked up because they are pretty rather than because they are needed to build something else. We will prove that you cannot stir a cup of coffee without leaving some point of the surface where it started, that every continuous function on an interval is a uniform limit of polynomials, that there are continuous functions which are differentiable nowhere at all, and that the number $\sum 10^{-n!}$ satisfies no polynomial equation with integer coefficients. Along the way we will meet a theorem which won its author a Nobel prize in economics.

What ties these together is a small number of extremely powerful ideas -- compactness, completeness, and the systematic exploitation of approximation. Almost every argument in the course is a variation on one of the following themes: a continuous function on a compact set attains its bounds; a sequence which does not move very much converges; and if you want to prove that an object with strange properties exists, it is often easier to prove that \emph{almost every} object has them than to write one down.

This article constitutes my notes for the `Topics in Analysis' course, held in Lent 2022 at Cambridge. These notes are \emph{not a transcription of the lectures}, and differ significantly in quite a few areas. Still, all lectured material should be covered.

\subsection{Metric Spaces and Completeness}

Almost all of the spaces we deal with in this course will be $\R^m$, $\C$, or a space of functions or of compact sets. The common framework is that of a metric space.

\begin{definition}[Metric Space]
	Let $X$ be a non-empty set. A function $d: X^2 \to \R$ is a \vocab{metric} on $X$ if
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item $d(x,y) \geq 0$ for all $x, y \in X$;
		\item $d(x,y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$;
		\item $d(x,y) = d(y,x)$ for all $x,y \in X$;
		\item $d(x,y) + d(y,z) \geq d(x,z)$ for all $x,y,z \in X$.
	\end{enumerate}
	We then call $(X,d)$ a \vocab{metric space}.
\end{definition}

The examples to keep in mind are Euclidean space $\R^m$ with $d(\vv{x},\vv{y}) = \norm{\vv{x}-\vv{y}}$, the complex plane with $d(z,w) = |z-w|$, and the space $C([a,b])$ of continuous real-valued functions on $[a,b]$ with the uniform metric $d(f,g) = \norm{f-g}_\infty$. We write
$$B(x,r) = \{y \in X : d(x,y) < r\}, \qquad \overline{B}(x,r) = \{y \in X : d(x,y) \leq r\}$$
for the open and closed balls of radius $r$ about $x$, and we say $E \subseteq X$ is \vocab{closed} if $x_n \in E$ and $x_n \to x$ imply $x \in E$, and $U \subseteq X$ is \vocab{open} if for each $u \in U$ there is a $\delta > 0$ with $B(u,\delta) \subseteq U$. These are complementary notions: $E$ is closed if and only if $X \setminus E$ is open.

The single most important property a metric space can have, for our purposes, is completeness.

\begin{definition}[Cauchy Sequence, Completeness]
	Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space. A sequence $(a_n)$ in $X$ is a \vocab{Cauchy sequence} if, given $\varepsilon > 0$, there is an $N$ with $d(a_n,a_m) < \varepsilon$ for all $n,m \geq N$. We say $(X,d)$ is \vocab{complete} if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges.
\end{definition}

We will need two small observations about Cauchy sequences over and over again, so it is worth isolating them.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cauchy-tricks}
	Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item If a Cauchy sequence $(x_n)$ has a subsequence converging to $x$, then $x_n \to x$.
		\item Suppose $\varepsilon_n > 0$ with $\sum_{n \geq 1} \varepsilon_n < \infty$. If every sequence $(x_n)$ in $X$ with $d(x_n, x_{n+1}) < \varepsilon_n$ for all $n$ converges, then $(X,d)$ is complete.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	(i) Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and pick $N$ with $d(x_n,x_m) < \varepsilon/2$ for $n,m \geq N$. If $x_{n(k)} \to x$, choose $k$ with $n(k) \geq N$ and $d(x_{n(k)}, x) < \varepsilon/2$. Then for $n \geq N$ we have $d(x_n, x) \leq d(x_n, x_{n(k)}) + d(x_{n(k)},x) < \varepsilon$.

	(ii) Let $(x_n)$ be Cauchy. Choose $n(1) < n(2) < \cdots$ so that $d(x_p, x_q) < \varepsilon_k$ whenever $p,q \geq n(k)$. Then the subsequence $y_k = x_{n(k)}$ satisfies $d(y_k,y_{k+1}) < \varepsilon_k$, so converges by hypothesis, and (i) finishes the job. \qedhere
\end{proof}

In practice we use (ii) with $\varepsilon_n = 2^{-n}$ or $\varepsilon_n = 100^{-n}$: to check completeness it suffices to deal with sequences whose steps shrink geometrically, which is a considerable convenience. We record without proof the fundamental example.

\begin{theorem}
	Euclidean space $\R^m$ with the usual metric is complete, as is $\C$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is the usual one: a Cauchy sequence in $\R^m$ is coordinatewise Cauchy, and $\R$ is complete by the general principle of convergence from Analysis I.

\subsection{Compactness in Euclidean Space}

In this course we deal only with subsets of $\R^m$, and there `compact' may be taken to mean `closed and bounded'. (The reader should be warned that this equivalence fails badly in general metric spaces, and that in the world of topological spaces one must further distinguish compactness from sequential compactness. We will not need any of this: if you intend to climb Everest you need your own oxygen supply, but for the Gog Magogs you do not.)

\begin{theorem}[Bolzano--Weierstrass]
	If $K > 0$ and $\vv{x}_r \in \R^m$ satisfy $\norm{\vv{x}_r} \leq K$ for all $r$, then there exist $\vv{x} \in \R^m$ and $r(1) < r(2) < \cdots$ such that $\vv{x}_{r(k)} \to \vv{x}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	For $m=1$ this is Analysis I. For general $m$, apply the one-dimensional result to the first coordinates to get a subsequence along which they converge, then pass to a further subsequence along which the second coordinates converge, and so on. After $m$ steps every coordinate converges, and convergence in $\R^m$ is coordinatewise.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:compact-bw}
	A subset $E$ of $\R^m$ is closed and bounded if and only if every sequence in $E$ has a subsequence converging to a limit in $E$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	If $E$ is closed and bounded, Bolzano--Weierstrass gives a convergent subsequence and closedness puts the limit in $E$. Conversely, if $E$ is unbounded, choose $\vv{x}_r \in E$ with $\norm{\vv{x}_r} \geq r$; no subsequence can converge. If $E$ is not closed, take $\vv{x}_r \in E$ with $\vv{x}_r \to \vv{x} \notin E$; every subsequence converges to $\vv{x} \notin E$.
\end{proof}

We shall call closed bounded subsets of $\R^m$ \vocab{compact}, and refer to the property in Theorem \ref{thm:compact-bw} as the Bolzano--Weierstrass property. Its usefulness comes from the following two results, which will be invoked so frequently that we shall often not bother to mention them.

\begin{theorem}
	If $E \subseteq \R^m$ is compact and $f : E \to \R^n$ is continuous, then $f(E)$ is compact.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Let $\vv{y}_r = f(\vv{x}_r)$ with $\vv{x}_r \in E$. Choose a subsequence $\vv{x}_{r(k)} \to \vv{x} \in E$. By continuity $\vv{y}_{r(k)} \to f(\vv{x}) \in f(E)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:attains-bounds}
	If $E \subseteq \R^m$ is non-empty and compact and $f : E \to \R$ is continuous, then there exist $\vv{a}, \vv{b} \in E$ with $f(\vv{b}) \leq f(\vv{x}) \leq f(\vv{a})$ for all $\vv{x} \in E$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	$f(E)$ is a non-empty compact subset of $\R$, hence bounded, and contains its supremum and infimum since it is closed.
\end{proof}

We will also want uniform continuity, which is compactness in another guise.

\begin{definition}[Uniform Continuity]
	Let $(X,d)$ and $(Y,\rho)$ be metric spaces. A function $f : X \to Y$ is \vocab{uniformly continuous} if, given $\varepsilon>0$, we can find $\delta>0$ such that $\rho(f(a),f(b)) < \varepsilon$ whenever $d(a,b) < \delta$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:unif-cts}
	If $E$ is a compact subset of $\R^m$ and $f : E \to \R^p$ is continuous, then $f$ is uniformly continuous.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	If not, there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ and points $\vv{a}_n, \vv{b}_n \in E$ with $\norm{\vv{a}_n - \vv{b}_n} < 1/n$ but $\norm{f(\vv{a}_n)-f(\vv{b}_n)} \geq \varepsilon$. Pass to a subsequence with $\vv{a}_{n(k)} \to \vv{a} \in E$; then $\vv{b}_{n(k)} \to \vv{a}$ too, so both $f(\vv{a}_{n(k)})$ and $f(\vv{b}_{n(k)})$ tend to $f(\vv{a})$, contradicting $\norm{f(\vv{a}_{n(k)})-f(\vv{b}_{n(k)})} \geq \varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

Finally, a piece of notation which will be with us all course. If $A$ is a non-empty subset of a metric space and $x$ a point, we write
$$d(x,A) = \inf\{d(x,a) : a \in A\}.$$

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:dist-cts}
	Let $A$ be a non-empty subset of a metric space $(X,d)$. Then $x \mapsto d(x,A)$ satisfies $|d(x,A)-d(y,A)| \leq d(x,y)$, and so is (uniformly) continuous. If $A$ is closed, then $d(x,A) = 0$ if and only if $x \in A$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	For any $a \in A$ we have $d(x,A) \leq d(x,a) \leq d(x,y)+d(y,a)$; taking the infimum over $a$ gives $d(x,A) \leq d(x,y) + d(y,A)$, and symmetry gives the Lipschitz bound. If $d(x,A)=0$ we can find $a_n \in A$ with $d(x,a_n) \to 0$, so $a_n \to x$ and $x \in A$ when $A$ is closed.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra}

Theorem \ref{thm:attains-bounds} looks like a piece of abstract nonsense, but it is enough to prove a theorem which -- contrary to its name -- is a theorem of analysis.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Algebra]\label{thm:fta}
	Every non-constant polynomial $P$ with complex coefficients has a root in $\C$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Write $P(z) = \sum_{j=0}^n a_j z^j$ with $n \geq 1$, and (dividing through) assume $a_n = 1$. Then for $z \neq 0$,
	$$|P(z)| \geq |z|^n\left(1 - |a_{n-1}||z|^{-1} - \cdots - |a_0||z|^{-n}\right),$$
	so if we set $R = 2\left(2 + \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|a_j|\right)$ then $|z| \geq R$ gives $|P(z)| \geq |z|^n/2 \geq R^n /2 > |a_0| = |P(0)|$.

	Now $\overline{B}(0,R)$ is compact and $|P|$ is continuous, so by Theorem \ref{thm:attains-bounds} there is a $z_0 \in \overline{B}(0,R)$ at which $|P|$ attains its minimum over $\overline{B}(0,R)$. By the previous paragraph $|P(z_0)| \leq |P(0)| < |P(z)|$ for all $|z| \geq R$, so in fact $z_0$ is a \emph{global} minimum of $|P|$ on $\C$.

	Suppose, for contradiction, that $P(z_0) \neq 0$. Replacing $P(z)$ by $P(z+z_0)$ we may assume $z_0 = 0$, and multiplying $P$ by a suitable unimodular constant we may assume $a_0 = P(0) > 0$. Let $m \geq 1$ be least with $a_m \neq 0$, so that
	$$P(z) = a_0 + a_m z^m + \sum_{j = m+1}^n a_j z^j.$$
	Choosing $\omega$ with $\omega^m = -|a_m|/a_m$ and replacing $P(z)$ by $P(\omega z)$, we may further assume $a_m < 0$. Now for real $\delta > 0$,
	$$\frac{P(\delta)-P(0)}{\delta^m} = a_m + \sum_{j=m+1}^{n} a_j \delta^{j-m} \to a_m < 0$$
	as $\delta \to 0^+$. So for small positive $\delta$ the real part of $P(\delta)-P(0)$ is negative and its imaginary part is $o(\delta^m)$, whence $|P(\delta)| < |P(0)|$. This contradicts the minimality of $|P(0)|$, so $P(z_0) = 0$.
\end{proof}

We shall meet a completely different proof of Theorem \ref{thm:fta}, using winding numbers, in the final section of the course. In the meantime we record the standard consequences, which we use freely.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:poly-roots}
	Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree $n$ over $\C$.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item If $P(a) = 0$ then $P(z) = (z-a)Q(z)$ for some polynomial $Q$ of degree $n-1$.
		\item $P(z) = A\prod_{j=1}^n (z-a_j)$ for some $A \neq 0$ and $a_j \in \C$.
		\item If $P$ has degree at most $n$ and vanishes at $n+1$ distinct points, then $P = 0$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	(i) By induction on $n$, division gives $P(z) = (z-a)Q(z) + r$ with $\deg Q = n-1$ and $r \in \C$; putting $z = a$ gives $r=0$. (ii) follows by induction using Theorem \ref{thm:fta} and (i). (iii) If $P \neq 0$ has degree $d \leq n$ then by (ii) it has at most $d \leq n$ roots.
\end{proof}

\section{Laplace's Equation}

Our first genuine topic is a piece of analysis which sits behind a great deal of the theory of partial differential equations. Recall that if $\phi$ is a real-valued function on (an open subset of) $\R^m$ with enough derivatives, we write
$$\nabla^2 \phi = \sum_{j=1}^m \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x_j^2},$$
and \vocab{Laplace's equation} is the equation $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$. Solutions are called \emph{harmonic}. In Part IA you cheerfully solved boundary value problems for Laplace's equation on discs and rectangles, and you may have been left with the impression that the Dirichlet problem
$$\nabla^2 \phi = 0 \ \text{ on } \Omega, \qquad \phi = f \ \text{ on } \partial\Omega$$
always has exactly one solution. We shall see that uniqueness holds in great generality, but that existence can fail.

We first need to recall three pieces of point-set vocabulary.

\begin{definition}[Interior, Closure, Boundary]
	Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space and $E \subseteq X$.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item The \vocab{interior} $\Int E$ is the set of $x \in X$ for which there is a $\delta>0$ with $B(x,\delta) \subseteq E$.
		\item The \vocab{closure} $\Cl E$ is the set of $x \in X$ for which there are $e_n \in E$ with $e_n \to x$.
		\item The \vocab{boundary} of $E$ is $\partial E = \Cl E \setminus \Int E$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

It is easy to check that $\Int E$ is the largest open set contained in $E$, that $\Cl E$ is the smallest closed set containing $E$, and hence that $\partial E = \Cl E \cap \Cl(X \setminus E)$ is closed. If $E \subseteq \R^m$ is bounded then so is $\Cl E$, so $\Cl E$ and $\partial E$ are compact.

\subsection{The Maximum Principle}

Throughout this subsection $\Omega$ is a non-empty bounded open subset of $\R^m$ and $\phi : \Cl \Omega \to \R$ is continuous and twice differentiable on $\Omega$. Note that $\Cl\Omega$ is compact and non-empty, so $\phi$ does attain a maximum somewhere; the content of what follows is \emph{where}.

The following lemma is really just the second derivative test, applied one coordinate at a time.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:strict-max}
	If $\nabla^2 \phi > 0$ everywhere on $\Omega$, then $\phi$ does not attain its maximum at any point of $\Omega$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Suppose $\phi$ attains its maximum over $\Cl\Omega$ at $\vv{x}^* \in \Omega$. Since $\Omega$ is open there is a $\delta>0$ with
	$$(x_1^*-\delta, x_1^*+\delta) \times \cdots \times (x_m^*-\delta,x_m^*+\delta) \subseteq \Omega.$$
	For each $j$ define $g_j(t) = \phi(x_1^*, \ldots, x_{j-1}^*, x_j^*+t, x_{j+1}^*,\ldots,x_m^*)$ for $|t|<\delta$. Then $g_j$ is twice differentiable with a maximum at $t=0$, so $g_j'(0) = 0$ and $g_j''(0) \leq 0$. But $g_j''(0) = \partial^2\phi/\partial x_j^2$ evaluated at $\vv{x}^*$, so summing over $j$ gives $\nabla^2\phi(\vv{x}^*) \leq 0$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis $\nabla^2 \phi > 0$ is of course much stronger than what we want. The trick, which is the same one used to deduce Rolle's theorem from the fact that an interior maximum is stationary, is to add a small strictly subharmonic perturbation and then let it go to zero.

\begin{theorem}[Maximum Principle]\label{thm:max-principle}
	If $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$ on $\Omega$, then $\phi$ attains its maximum over $\Cl\Omega$ at some point of $\partial\Omega$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	For $n \geq 1$ set $\phi_n(\vv{x}) = \phi(\vv{x}) + \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^m x_j^2$. Then $\nabla^2 \phi_n = 2m/n > 0$ on $\Omega$, so by Lemma \ref{lem:strict-max} the maximum of $\phi_n$ over $\Cl\Omega$ is attained at some $\vv{x}_n^* \in \partial\Omega$. Since $\partial \Omega$ is compact, we may pass to a subsequence $\vv{x}_{n(j)}^* \to \vv{x}^* \in \partial\Omega$.

	Let $R$ be such that $\norm{\vv{x}} \leq R$ for all $\vv{x} \in \Cl\Omega$. For every $\vv{x} \in \Cl\Omega$ we have
	$$\phi(\vv{x}_{n(j)}^*) + \frac{R^2}{n(j)} \geq \phi_{n(j)}(\vv{x}_{n(j)}^*) \geq \phi_{n(j)}(\vv{x}) \geq \phi(\vv{x}).$$
	Letting $j \to \infty$ and using continuity of $\phi$ gives $\phi(\vv{x}^*) \geq \phi(\vv{x})$, as required.
\end{proof}

Applying the theorem to $-\phi$ shows that the minimum is also attained on the boundary, and uniqueness follows at once.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness for the Dirichlet Problem]\label{thm:dirichlet-unique}
	Suppose $\phi,\psi : \Cl\Omega \to \R$ are continuous, twice differentiable on $\Omega$, and satisfy $\nabla^2\phi = \nabla^2\psi = 0$ on $\Omega$. If $\phi = \psi$ on $\partial\Omega$, then $\phi = \psi$ on $\Cl\Omega$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Set $\chi = \phi-\psi$. Then $\nabla^2 \chi = 0$ on $\Omega$ and $\chi = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$, so by Theorem \ref{thm:max-principle} the maximum of $\chi$ is $0$, and applying the same to $-\chi$ gives that the minimum is $0$. Hence $\chi \equiv 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	The same argument gives a maximum principle for analytic functions. If $\Omega \subseteq \C$ is bounded and open and $f : \Cl\Omega \to \C$ is continuous and analytic on $\Omega$, then $\re f$ and $\im f$ are harmonic, and one deduces that $|f|$ attains its maximum on $\partial\Omega$: given $z_0$, rotate $f$ so that $\re f(z_0) = |f(z_0)|$ and apply Theorem \ref{thm:max-principle} to $\re f$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Zaremba's Example}

So much for uniqueness. What about existence? The following example, due to Zaremba, shows that we cannot expect a solution for every bounded open $\Omega$ and every continuous boundary datum. It is a nice illustration of how uniqueness theorems can be used to \emph{destroy} existence.

\begin{example}[Zaremba]
	Let $D = \{\vv{x} \in \R^2 : \norm{\vv{x}} < 1\}$ and let $\Omega = D \setminus \{\vv{0}\}$ be the punctured disc. Then $\Omega$ is open and bounded, $\Cl\Omega = \Cl D$, and
	$$\partial\Omega = \{\vv{x} : \norm{\vv{x}} = 1\} \cup \{\vv{0}\}.$$
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.1]
			\fill[definitionbg] (0,0) circle (1.6);
			\draw[thick] (0,0) circle (1.6);
			\fill[white] (0,0) circle (0.055);
			\draw[thick] (0,0) circle (0.055);
			\node at (0.75,0.55) {$\Omega$};
			\node[below] at (0,-0.16) {\small $\vv{0}$};
			\node[right] at (1.62,0.55) {\small $\phi = 0$};
			\node[left] at (-0.12,0.28) {\small $\phi=1$};
			\draw[->,gray] (-0.35,0.5) -- (-0.07,0.09);
		\end{tikzpicture}
	\end{center}
	We claim that there is no continuous $\phi : \Cl\Omega \to \R$, twice differentiable on $\Omega$, with $\nabla^2\phi = 0$ on $\Omega$ and
	$$\phi(\vv{x}) = \begin{cases} 0 & \norm{\vv{x}} = 1, \\ 1 & \vv{x} = \vv{0}.\end{cases}$$

	Suppose such a $\phi$ existed. Let $R_\theta$ be the rotation of $\R^2$ through $\theta$ about the origin. Then $\vv{x} \mapsto \phi(R_\theta \vv{x})$ is also continuous on $\Cl \Omega$, harmonic on $\Omega$, and takes the \emph{same} boundary values (since the boundary data are rotation-invariant). By the uniqueness Theorem \ref{thm:dirichlet-unique} we get $\phi = \phi \circ R_\theta$ for every $\theta$, so $\phi$ is radially symmetric: $\phi(\vv{x}) = f(\norm{\vv{x}})$ for some $f : [0,1] \to \R$.

	Writing $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and computing,
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} &= \frac{x}{r}f'(r), \\
		\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} &= \left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{x^2}{r^3}\right)f'(r) + \frac{x^2}{r^2}f''(r),
	\end{align*}
	and similarly for $y$, so that adding gives the familiar radial form
	$$\nabla^2 \phi = f''(r) + \frac{1}{r}f'(r) = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\big(rf'(r)\big).$$
	Thus $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}(rf'(r)) = 0$ for $0 < r <1$, so $rf'(r) = B$ and $f(r) = A + B\log r$ for constants $A,B$. But $\phi$ is continuous at $\vv{0}$, and $A + B\log r$ has a limit as $r \to 0^+$ only when $B = 0$. So $f$ is constant, and $\phi$ cannot take the value $0$ on $\norm{\vv{x}}=1$ and $1$ at $\vv{0}$. This is the contradiction we wanted.
\end{example}

The moral is that the boundary point $\vv{0}$ is `too thin' for the boundary condition there to be felt. Lebesgue later produced a three-dimensional example (the \emph{Lebesgue thorn}) in which the offending boundary point is a cusp rather than an isolated point, so that the failure is not simply a matter of the boundary having an isolated point. Deciding exactly which boundary points are `regular' in this sense is a substantial piece of potential theory, and we shall not pursue it.

\section{Fixed Point Theorems}

In Analysis I we proved the intermediate value theorem, and used it to prove a very pretty one-dimensional fixed point theorem.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:1d-fixed}
	If $f : [a,b] \to [a,b]$ is continuous, then there is a $w \in [a,b]$ with $f(w) = w$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Set $g(x) = f(x)-x$. Then $g$ is continuous, $g(a) = f(a)-a \geq 0$ and $g(b) = f(b) - b \leq 0$. By the intermediate value theorem there is a $w \in [a,b]$ with $g(w) = 0$.
\end{proof}

The picture is irresistible: the graph of $f$ lives in the square $[a,b]^2$, it starts on or above the diagonal and ends on or below it, so it must cross.

	\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

The implication can be reversed: Theorem \ref{thm:1d-fixed} implies the intermediate value theorem, and both are equivalent to the statement that $[0,1]$ is connected. This is worth bearing in mind, because the object of this section is to prove the two-dimensional analogue, and the proof will consist almost entirely of showing that a chain of superficially unrelated statements are all equivalent to one another.

\begin{theorem}[Brouwer's Fixed Point Theorem]\label{thm:brouwer}
	Let $\dbar = \{\vv{x} \in \R^2 : \norm{\vv{x}} \leq 1\}$. If $f : \dbar \to \dbar$ is continuous, then there is a $\vv{w} \in \dbar$ with $f(\vv{w}) = \vv{w}$.
\end{theorem}

We shall keep strictly to two dimensions, but the reader should know that the theorem and most of its consequences hold in $\R^n$; the only ingredient which needs real work to generalise is Sperner's lemma, and even there the changes are cosmetic rather than conceptual.

The result is not restricted to discs. The following observation lets us move it around freely, and we shall use it without comment.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:homeo-transfer}
	Suppose $g : \dbar \to A$ is a bijection with $g$ and $g^{-1}$ continuous. If $F : A \to A$ is continuous, then $F$ has a fixed point.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	The map $g^{-1} \circ F \circ g : \dbar \to \dbar$ is continuous, so by Theorem \ref{thm:brouwer} has a fixed point $\vv{w}$. Then $g(\vv{w})$ is a fixed point of $F$.
\end{proof}

In particular Brouwer's theorem holds for closed squares, closed triangles, and any other set homeomorphic to a disc. Throughout we write
$$D = \{\vv{x} \in \R^2 : \norm{\vv{x}}<1\}, \quad \dbar = \{\vv{x}: \norm{\vv{x}}\leq 1\}, \quad \partial D = \{\vv{x} : \norm{\vv{x}}=1\}.$$

\subsection{The No Retraction Theorem}

The first equivalence is the one which explains why the theorem is true: a fixed-point-free map lets you comb the disc onto its boundary, and that is something you cannot do.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:no-retract}
	The following are equivalent.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item Every continuous $f : \dbar \to \dbar$ has a fixed point.
		\item There is no continuous $g : \dbar \to \partial D$ with $g(\vv{x}) = \vv{x}$ for all $\vv{x} \in \partial D$. (Such a $g$ is called a \vocab{retraction}.)
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Suppose (i) holds and $g : \dbar \to \partial D$ is a retraction. Let $R$ be rotation through $\pi$ about $\vv{0}$. Then $R \circ g : \dbar \to \dbar$ is continuous, so has a fixed point $\vv{w}$. But $R(g(\vv{w})) = \vv{w}$ forces $\vv{w} \in R(\partial D) = \partial D$, and then $g(\vv{w}) = \vv{w}$, so $R\vv{w} = \vv{w}$, which is false for $\vv{w} \neq \vv{0}$. Since $\vv{0} \notin \partial D$ we have a contradiction, and (ii) holds.

	Conversely, suppose (ii) holds and $f : \dbar \to \dbar$ is continuous with no fixed point. For $\vv{x} \neq \vv{y}$ in $\dbar$ let $F(\vv{x},\vv{y})$ be the point where the ray starting at $\vv{y}$ and passing through $\vv{x}$ meets $\partial D$; explicitly, writing $\vv{u} = (\vv{x}-\vv{y})/\norm{\vv{x}-\vv{y}}$, we have $F(\vv{x},\vv{y}) = \vv{x} + t\vv{u}$ where $t \geq 0$ is the unique non-negative root of $\norm{\vv{x}+t\vv{u}}^2 = 1$, that is
	$$t = -\vv{x}\cdot\vv{u} + \sqrt{(\vv{x}\cdot\vv{u})^2 + 1 - \norm{\vv{x}}^2}.$$
	This is a continuous function of $(\vv{x},\vv{y})$ on $\{(\vv{x},\vv{y}) \in \dbar^2 : \vv{x} \neq \vv{y}\}$. Set $g(\vv{x}) = F(\vv{x},f(\vv{x}))$, which makes sense since $f(\vv{x}) \neq \vv{x}$ everywhere. Then $g$ is continuous, $g(\dbar) \subseteq \partial D$, and if $\vv{x} \in \partial D$ then $\vv{x}$ itself is already on the ray and on $\partial D$, so $g(\vv{x}) = \vv{x}$. Thus $g$ is a retraction, contradicting (ii).
\end{proof}

\subsection{From Retractions to Colourings}

The next step replaces the circle by a triangle and the retraction by a map which preserves three closed pieces of the boundary. Let $\vv{a}_1,\vv{a}_2,\vv{a}_3$ be unit vectors making angles of $2\pi/3$ with each other, let $T$ be the closed triangle with these vertices, and let $I, J, K$ be its three closed sides. Correspondingly let
$$\tilde I = \{(\cos\theta,\sin\theta): 0 \leq \theta \leq \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\}, \quad \tilde J = \{(\cos\theta,\sin\theta): \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\leq\theta\leq\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\},$$
and $\tilde K = \{(\cos\theta,\sin\theta) : \frac{4\pi}{3}\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\}$ be the three corresponding closed arcs of $\partial D$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:three-arcs}
	The following three statements are equivalent.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item There is no retraction from $\dbar$ to $\partial D$.
		\item There is no continuous $\tilde k : \dbar \to \partial D$ with $\tilde k(\tilde I) \subseteq \tilde I$, $\tilde k(\tilde J) \subseteq \tilde J$ and $\tilde k(\tilde K) \subseteq \tilde K$.
		\item There is no continuous $k : T \to \partial T$ with $k(I)\subseteq I$, $k(J) \subseteq J$ and $k(K)\subseteq K$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	(ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i) is immediate, since a retraction certainly maps each arc into itself. For (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii), suppose $\tilde k$ is as in (ii) and let $R$ be rotation through $\pi$. Then $R\circ \tilde k : \dbar \to \dbar$ has no fixed point: a fixed point $\vv{w}$ would have to lie on $\partial D$, and $\vv{w}$ lies in one of the three arcs, say $\tilde I$, so $\tilde k(\vv{w}) \in \tilde I$ while $R\tilde k(\vv{w}) = \vv{w} \in \tilde I$ means $\tilde k (\vv{w}) = R\vv{w}$, which lies in the arc antipodal to the one containing $\vv{w}$. Since an arc of length $2\pi/3$ is disjoint from its antipodal image, this is impossible. By Theorem \ref{thm:no-retract} this contradicts (i).

	For the equivalence of (ii) and (iii), define $\Phi : \dbar \to T$ by $\Phi(\vv{0}) = \vv{0}$ and, for $\vv{x} \neq \vv{0}$, $\Phi(\vv{x}) = \rho(\vv{x})\vv{x}$ where $\rho(\vv{x})>0$ is chosen so that $\rho(\vv{x})\vv{x} \in \partial T$ when $\norm{\vv{x}}=1$; in other words we stretch each radius of the disc onto the corresponding radius of the triangle. Since $\vv{0}$ is interior to $T$ and $\partial T$ meets each ray from $\vv{0}$ exactly once, $\rho$ is a continuous positive function of the direction of $\vv{x}$ alone, and $\Phi$ is a homeomorphism carrying $\partial D$ onto $\partial T$ and $\tilde I, \tilde J,\tilde K$ onto $I, J, K$ respectively. So $\tilde k \mapsto \Phi\circ\tilde k\circ\Phi^{-1}$ is a bijection between maps of the type in (ii) and maps of the type in (iii).
\end{proof}

The next equivalence is the crucial one: it converts a statement about continuous maps into a statement about closed sets, and closed sets are things we can attack combinatorially.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:three-colours}
	The following are equivalent.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item There is no continuous $h : T \to \partial T$ with $h(I)\subseteq I$, $h(J)\subseteq J$, $h(K)\subseteq K$.
		\item If $A, B, C$ are closed subsets of $T$ with $A \supseteq I$, $B\supseteq J$, $C \supseteq K$ and $A\cup B\cup C = T$, then $A \cap B \cap C \neq \emptyset$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Suppose (ii) holds and $h$ is as in (i). Put $A = h^{-1}(I)$, $B = h^{-1}(J)$, $C = h^{-1}(K)$. These are closed (preimages of closed sets under a continuous map), they cover $T$ since $I \cup J\cup K = \partial T$, and $A \supseteq I$ since $h(I)\subseteq I$; similarly for $B$ and $C$. But $I \cap J \cap K = \emptyset$ (the three sides have no common point), so $A\cap B\cap C = h^{-1}(I\cap J\cap K) = \emptyset$, contradicting (ii).

	Conversely, suppose (i) holds and $A,B,C$ are closed sets as in (ii) with $A\cap B\cap C = \emptyset$. It is convenient to realise $T$ in barycentric coordinates,\footnote{Barycentric coordinates were invented by M\"obius, and are exactly the right language for everything in this section.} identifying it with
	$$T = \{(x,y,z) \in \R^3 : x,y,z\geq 0,\ x+y+z = 1\},$$
	with $I = \{x = 0\}\cap T$, $J = \{y=0\}\cap T$ and $K = \{z=0\}\cap T$. Define
	$$h(\vv{x}) = \frac{1}{d(\vv{x},A)+d(\vv{x},B)+d(\vv{x},C)}\big(d(\vv{x},A),\, d(\vv{x},B),\, d(\vv{x},C)\big).$$
	The denominator never vanishes: if it did, then $\vv{x}$ would be at zero distance from each of the closed sets $A,B,C$, so $\vv{x} \in A\cap B\cap C = \emptyset$. By Lemma \ref{lem:dist-cts} each numerator is continuous, so $h$ is continuous, and clearly $h(\vv{x}) \in T$ with at least one coordinate zero (as $A\cup B\cup C = T$ means every $\vv{x}$ lies in one of them), so $h(T)\subseteq \partial T$. Finally if $\vv{x} \in I$ then $\vv{x}\in A$, so $d(\vv{x},A)=0$ and $h(\vv{x}) \in I$; similarly for $J$ and $K$. This contradicts (i).
\end{proof}

Putting Theorem \ref{thm:no-retract} and Lemmas \ref{lem:three-arcs} and \ref{lem:three-colours} together,
$$\text{Brouwer} \iff \text{no retraction} \iff \text{three arcs} \iff \text{three sides} \iff \text{three colours},$$
so it is enough to prove the three colours statement. That is what we now do, and the engine is a purely combinatorial lemma of Sperner.

\subsection{Sperner's Lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Sperner]\label{lem:sperner}
	Consider a triangle $DEF$ divided into a triangular grid by $n-1$ equally spaced lines parallel to each side. Colour every vertex of the grid red, green or blue in such a way that every vertex on the side $DE$ except $E$ is red, every vertex on $EF$ except $F$ is green, and every vertex on $FD$ except $D$ is blue. Then some small triangle of the grid has all three of its vertices differently coloured.
\end{lemma}

In fact the proof gives more: the number of such `rainbow' triangles is odd. Here is a grid with $n=4$; three of the small triangles are rainbow.

\begin{center}
	\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.74]
		% grid lines
		\foreach \j in {0,1,2,3,4} {\draw[thin,gray] ({0.75*\j},{1.299*\j}) -- ({6-0.75*\j},{1.299*\j});}
		\foreach \i in {0,1,2,3,4} {\draw[thin,gray] ({1.5*\i},0) -- ({1.5*\i+0.75*(4-\i)},{1.299*(4-\i)});}
		\foreach \c in {0,1,2,3,4} {\draw[thin,gray] ({1.5*\c},0) -- ({0.75*\c},{1.299*\c});}
		% rainbow triangles
		\fill[yellow!45] (2.25,1.299) -- (3.75,1.299) -- (3,2.598) -- cycle;
		\fill[yellow!45] (1.5,0) -- (0.75,1.299) -- (2.25,1.299) -- cycle;
		\fill[yellow!45] (3.75,1.299) -- (3,2.598) -- (4.5,2.598) -- cycle;
		\draw[thick] (0,0) -- (6,0) -- (3,5.196) -- cycle;
		% vertices
		\foreach \p in {(0,0),(1.5,0),(3,0),(4.5,0),(3.75,1.299)} {\fill[red] \p circle (3.2pt);}
		\foreach \p in {(6,0),(5.25,1.299),(4.5,2.598),(3.75,3.897),(2.25,1.299)} {\fill[OliveGreen] \p circle (3.2pt);}
		\foreach \p in {(3,5.196),(2.25,3.897),(1.5,2.598),(0.75,1.299),(3,2.598)} {\fill[blue] \p circle (3.2pt);}
		\node[below left] at (0,0) {\small $D$};
		\node[below right] at (6,0) {\small $E$};
		\node[above] at (3,5.196) {\small $F$};
	\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{proof}
	Give each side of each small triangle a value, according to the following rule. Traversing the side in a fixed rotational sense (say clockwise) around its small triangle, set
	$$\theta(RG) = \theta(GB) = \theta(BR) = 1, \qquad \theta(GR)=\theta(BG)=\theta(RB) = -1,$$
	and $\theta = 0$ on a side whose two endpoints have the same colour. Note that reversing the direction of travel changes the sign of $\theta$.

	For a small triangle $\Delta$ let $\psi(\Delta)$ be the sum of the values of its three sides, traversed clockwise. If the three vertices of $\Delta$ are not all different then two of them agree, and a moment's checking shows $\psi(\Delta) = 0$: for example a triangle coloured $R,R,G$ contributes $0 + 1 + (-1) = 0$. If the vertices are all different then going round clockwise we see either $R,G,B$ or $R,B,G$, giving $\psi(\Delta) = 3$ or $\psi(\Delta) = -3$. In particular $\psi(\Delta) \neq 0$ exactly when $\Delta$ is rainbow, and $\psi(\Delta) \equiv 3 \pmod 6$ in that case.

	Now sum $\psi(\Delta)$ over all small triangles $\Delta$. Every interior side of the grid belongs to exactly two small triangles, and is traversed in opposite directions in the two, so its two contributions cancel. Hence
	$$\sum_\Delta \psi(\Delta) = \sum_{\text{sides on }\partial(DEF)} \theta(\text{side}),$$
	the boundary sides being traversed clockwise around the big triangle.

	Consider the side $DE$. All its grid vertices are red except the last, $E$, which is green. So travelling from $D$ to $E$ we see $R,R,\ldots,R,G$ and the total is $\theta(RG) = 1$. Similarly $EF$ reads $G,G,\ldots,G,B$ contributing $1$, and $FD$ reads $B,B,\ldots,B,R$ contributing $1$. Hence $\sum_\Delta \psi(\Delta) = 3 \neq 0$, so at least one small triangle is rainbow. Indeed, since each rainbow triangle contributes $\pm3$ and the total is $3$, the number of rainbow triangles is odd.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma \ref{lem:three-colours} (ii), and hence of Brouwer's theorem]
	Let $A,B,C$ be closed subsets of $T$ with $A \supseteq I$, $B \supseteq J$, $C \supseteq K$ and $A \cup B \cup C = T$, where we label the sides so that $I = DE$, $J = EF$ and $K = FD$.

	Fix $n$ and subdivide $T$ into a triangular grid with $n-1$ lines parallel to each side, so that each small triangle has diameter at most $\mathrm{diam}(T)/n$. Colour each grid vertex $\vv{v}$ as follows: if $\vv{v} \in A$ colour it red, otherwise if $\vv{v}\in B$ colour it green, otherwise colour it blue (which is legitimate as $A\cup B\cup C = T$). Since $I \subseteq A$, every vertex on $I$ is red; since $J \subseteq B$, every vertex of $J$ not already coloured red is green -- and the only vertex of $J$ lying in $I$ is the shared corner $E$. Rather than fuss over corners, note that we are free to recolour the three corners: $D$ red, $E$ green, $F$ blue is consistent with $D \in I \subseteq A$, $E \in J \subseteq B$, $F \in K \subseteq C$. Thus the hypotheses of Sperner's lemma hold.

	So for each $n$ we obtain a small triangle with vertices $\vv{a}_n$ (red, so in $A$), $\vv{b}_n$ (green, so in $B$) and $\vv{c}_n$ (blue, so in $C$), with
	$$\norm{\vv{a}_n - \vv{b}_n},\ \norm{\vv{b}_n-\vv{c}_n},\ \norm{\vv{c}_n-\vv{a}_n} \leq \frac{\mathrm{diam}(T)}{n}.$$
	As $T$ is compact we may pass to a subsequence along which $\vv{a}_{n(j)} \to \vv{x}$ for some $\vv{x}\in T$. Since the three points of each triple are within $\mathrm{diam}(T)/n$ of each other, $\vv{b}_{n(j)} \to \vv{x}$ and $\vv{c}_{n(j)}\to\vv{x}$ too. As $A, B, C$ are closed, $\vv{x} \in A\cap B\cap C$, which is therefore non-empty.
\end{proof}

The reader who is worried about the one-dimensional analogue may like to check that the whole of this section degenerates, when $T$ is replaced by $[0,1]$ and three colours by two, into the following statement: if we colour $0,1/n,\ldots,1$ red or blue with $0$ red and $1$ blue, then some pair of neighbours have different colours. That statement proves the intermediate value theorem, and Sperner's lemma is exactly its two-dimensional cousin.

\subsection{Two Applications}

Brouwer's theorem is deep, and it is worth seeing that it does real work. Here is a result which the Markov chains course makes one want very much.

\begin{proposition}[Perron--Frobenius, stochastic case]\label{prop:perron}
	Let $A = (a_{ij})$ be an $n\times n$ matrix with $a_{ij}\geq 0$ for all $i,j$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ij} = 1$ for each $j$. Then $A$ has an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$ lying in
	$$\Sigma = \Big\{\vv{x} \in \R^n : x_j \geq 0 \text{ for all } j, \ \textstyle\sum_j x_j = 1\Big\}.$$
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	If $\vv{x} \in \Sigma$ then $(A\vv{x})_i = \sum_j a_{ij}x_j \geq 0$ and
	$$\sum_i (A\vv{x})_i = \sum_j x_j \sum_i a_{ij} = \sum_j x_j = 1,$$
	so $A\Sigma \subseteq \Sigma$. Now $\Sigma$ is a closed bounded convex subset of the hyperplane $\sum_j x_j = 1$ with non-empty interior in that hyperplane, so is homeomorphic to a closed disc of dimension $n-1$ (radially, as in Lemma \ref{lem:three-arcs}). Applying Brouwer's theorem in dimension $n-1$ via Lemma \ref{lem:homeo-transfer}, the continuous map $\vv{x}\mapsto A\vv{x}$ has a fixed point $\vv{x}^* \in \Sigma$, and $A\vv{x}^* = \vv{x}^*$ is the required eigenvector.
\end{proof}

Reading `$i$' and `$j$' the other way round, this says that every finite state Markov chain has an invariant distribution -- a result which is unpleasant to prove by bare hands, and which becomes almost obvious once we are allowed a fixed point theorem.

The second application is a statement which is `obvious' in the worst sense of the word.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:crossing}
	Let $ABCD$ be a square. If $\gamma$ is a continuous path in the square joining $A$ to $C$, and $\tau$ is a continuous path in the square joining $B$ to $D$, then $\gamma$ and $\tau$ intersect.
\end{proposition}

We shall not give the details (they form a rather fiddly exercise), but the idea is worth recording: take the square to be $[-1,1]^2$ with $\gamma, \tau : [0,1] \to [-1,1]^2$, and suppose $\gamma(s) \neq \tau(t)$ for all $s,t$. Then
$$F(s,t) = \frac{\big(\tau_1(t)-\gamma_1(s),\ \gamma_2(s)-\tau_2(t)\big)}{\norm{\gamma(s)-\tau(t)}_\infty}$$
defines a continuous map of $[0,1]^2$ into itself, and one checks that a fixed point of $F$ would have to lie in a corner, where the boundary conditions on $\gamma$ and $\tau$ make it impossible. This is a baby case of the Jordan curve theorem, which asserts that a simple closed curve in the plane divides it into exactly two components. The Jordan curve theorem is genuinely hard, and is not part of this course; but it is comforting to know that its plausibility is not a delusion.

\section{Non-Zero Sum Games}

It is said that converting the front garden of a house into a parking space raises the value of that house and lowers the value of every other house on the road. Once everybody has done the conversion, every house is worth less than it was before anybody started. Situations of this kind -- where individually rational choices produce a collectively poor outcome -- are the meat of game theory, and it turns out that Brouwer's theorem has a great deal to say about them.

Let us set up the simplest possible model. Albert has a choice between actions $A_1$ and $A_2$, and Bertha between $B_1$ and $B_2$. If Albert plays $A_i$ and Bertha plays $B_j$, then Albert receives $a_{ij}$ and Bertha receives $b_{ij}$. In IB Optimisation you studied the \emph{zero-sum} case $a_{ij} = -b_{ij}$, where one player's gain is the other's loss; we shall make no such assumption.

Suppose Albert plays $A_i$ with probability $p_i$ and Bertha plays $B_j$ with probability $q_j$, where $p_1+p_2 = q_1+q_2 = 1$ and all entries are non-negative. Writing $\vv{p} = (p_1,p_2)$ and $\vv{q} = (q_1,q_2)$, the expected payoffs are
$$A(\vv{p},\vv{q}) = \sum_{i=1}^2\sum_{j=1}^2 a_{ij}p_iq_j, \qquad B(\vv{p},\vv{q}) = \sum_{i=1}^2\sum_{j=1}^2 b_{ij}p_iq_j.$$
In the zero-sum case you saw that there is a pair of strategies such that neither player would change their choice even knowing the other's. Away from zero-sum we can imagine an endless dance: Albert chooses $\vv{p}$, Bertha responds with $\vv{q}$, Albert switches to $\vv{p}'$, Bertha to $\vv{q}'$, and so on. Does the dance ever stop?

\begin{definition}[Nash Equilibrium]
	A pair $(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*)$ of strategies is a \vocab{Nash equilibrium} (or \vocab{Nash stable point}) if
	$$A(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*) \geq A(\vv{p},\vv{q}^*) \quad\text{and}\quad B(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*)\geq B(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q})$$
	for all strategies $\vv{p},\vv{q}$. In other words, neither player can do better by unilaterally changing their strategy.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Nash]\label{thm:nash}
	For any real numbers $a_{ij}, b_{ij}$ there exists a Nash equilibrium.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Write $\vv{e}_1 = (1,0)$ and $\vv{e}_2 = (0,1)$ for the two pure strategies, and let
	$$\Gamma = \{(\vv{p},\vv{q}) : p_1,p_2,q_1,q_2\geq 0,\ p_1+p_2 = q_1+q_2 = 1\} \subseteq \R^4,$$
	which is (a copy of) the square $[0,1]^2$, and so closed, bounded and homeomorphic to a disc.

	For $(\vv{p},\vv{q}) \in \Gamma$ define
	$$u_i(\vv{p},\vv{q}) = \max\{A(\vv{e}_i,\vv{q}) - A(\vv{p},\vv{q}),\, 0\}, \qquad v_j(\vv{p},\vv{q}) = \max\{B(\vv{p},\vv{f}_j)-B(\vv{p},\vv{q}),\,0\},$$
	where $\vv{f}_j$ are the pure strategies for Bertha. Thus $u_i > 0$ says `against $\vv{q}$, Albert would do strictly better playing $A_i$ outright'. These are continuous, being maxima of continuous functions. Now set $F(\vv{p},\vv{q}) = (\vv{p}',\vv{q}')$ where
	$$p_i' = \frac{p_i + u_i(\vv{p},\vv{q})}{1+u_1(\vv{p},\vv{q})+u_2(\vv{p},\vv{q})}, \qquad q_j' = \frac{q_j+v_j(\vv{p},\vv{q})}{1+v_1(\vv{p},\vv{q})+v_2(\vv{p},\vv{q})}.$$
	The denominators are at least $1$, the numerators are non-negative, and $p_1'+p_2' = 1 = q_1'+q_2'$, so $F : \Gamma \to \Gamma$ is a continuous map of $\Gamma$ into itself. By Brouwer's theorem (in the form of Lemma \ref{lem:homeo-transfer}) it has a fixed point $(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*)$.

	We claim this is a Nash equilibrium. Write $u_i = u_i(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*)$. Being a fixed point means $p_i^*(1+u_1+u_2) = p_i^* + u_i$, that is
	$$p_i^*(u_1+u_2) = u_i \qquad (i=1,2).$$
	Since $A(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*) = p_1^*A(\vv{e}_1,\vv{q}^*)+p_2^*A(\vv{e}_2,\vv{q}^*)$ is a convex combination, we cannot have $A(\vv{e}_i,\vv{q}^*)>A(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*)$ for both $i$. So at least one $u_i$ vanishes; without loss of generality $u_2 = 0$. Then $p_2^*(u_1+u_2) = 0$, so $p_2^*u_1 = 0$. If $u_1 > 0$ then $p_2^* = 0$, so $p_1^*=1$ and $\vv{p}^* = \vv{e}_1$, whence $u_1 = \max\{A(\vv{e}_1,\vv{q}^*)-A(\vv{e}_1,\vv{q}^*),0\} = 0$, a contradiction. So $u_1 = u_2 = 0$.

	Thus $A(\vv{e}_i,\vv{q}^*)\leq A(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*)$ for $i=1,2$, and so for any strategy $\vv{p}$,
	$$A(\vv{p},\vv{q}^*) = p_1A(\vv{e}_1,\vv{q}^*)+p_2A(\vv{e}_2,\vv{q}^*) \leq A(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*).$$
	The same argument applied to $v_j$ gives $B(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q})\leq B(\vv{p}^*,\vv{q}^*)$ for all $\vv{q}$.
\end{proof}

Nothing in the argument really used the number $2$: with $m$ players having finitely many choices each, exactly the same construction (in a product of simplices) together with Brouwer's theorem in the appropriate dimension gives a stable point. Note also that the IB theory applied only to two players and only to zero-sum games; Nash's theorem is a considerable strengthening.

Two warnings. First, equilibria need not be unique. If Albert and Bertha each choose `scissors' or `paper', receiving \pounds 1 each if both choose scissors, \pounds 2 each if both choose paper, and nothing if they disagree, then both `everyone plays scissors' and `everyone plays paper' are stable. Second, and more disturbingly, a unique equilibrium may be very bad for everyone concerned. That, however, is not the mathematician's problem.

\begin{example}[Chicken]
	Albert and Bartholomew drive their cars at one another. If both swerve, each loses $1$ prestige point. If exactly one swerves, the swerver loses $5$ points and the non-swerver gains $10$. If neither swerves, both lose $100$.

	Let $p$ be the probability that Albert swerves and $q$ the probability that Bartholomew does. Then
	$$A(p,q) = -pq - 5p(1-q) + 10(1-p)q - 100(1-p)(1-q) = p(95 - 106q) + 110q - 100,$$
	and by symmetry $B(p,q) = q(95-106p) + 110p - 100$.

	The recipe for problems like this is: first look at the interior of the square, then at the interiors of the edges, then at the vertices. In the interior we need $\partial A/\partial p = 0$ and $\partial B/\partial q = 0$, that is $95 - 106q = 0$ and $95-106p=0$, giving the interior equilibrium
	$$(p^*,q^*) = \left(\frac{95}{106},\frac{95}{106}\right).$$
	On the edge $p = 1$ we have $\partial B/\partial q = 95-106 = -11 < 0$, so Bartholomew's best response is $q = 0$; and when $q = 0$ we have $\partial A/\partial p = 95 > 0$, so Albert's best response is indeed $p=1$. Hence $(1,0)$ is an equilibrium (`Albert always swerves, Bartholomew never does'), and by symmetry so is $(0,1)$. At $(1,1)$ we have $\partial A /\partial p = -11 <0$ and at $(0,0)$ we have $\partial A/\partial p = 95 > 0$, so neither is stable. So there are exactly three Nash equilibria.
\end{example}

It is genuinely easy to solve toy problems in this way; the difficulty is that with $m$ players we must examine the interior of the cube, then each face, then each edge, then each vertex, and the number of steps explodes. So far as anybody knows, this curse of dimensionality is unavoidable.

\section{Dividing the Pot}

Faced with the previous section, the tender-hearted ask `why not cooperate?'. Even granting that people sometimes do, a question remains: how should the gains from cooperation be divided? Nash gave a striking answer.

\begin{definition}[Convex Set]
	A subset $E$ of $\R^m$ is \vocab{convex} if $\lambda\vv{u} + (1-\lambda)\vv{v} \in E$ whenever $\vv{u},\vv{v}\in E$ and $0\leq \lambda\leq 1$.
\end{definition}

The set-up is as follows. There are $m$ participants who must jointly choose a point $\vv{x}$ of a closed, bounded, convex set $E \subseteq \R^m$; the value of the outcome to the $j$th participant is $x_j$. Convexity is not an arbitrary assumption: if the participants can agree on $\vv{u}$ or on $\vv{v}$, they can agree to toss a suitable coin, and the expected outcome is then $\lambda\vv{u}+(1-\lambda)\vv{v}$. They also know a \vocab{status quo} point $\vv{s} \in E$, the outcome if they fail to agree.

Nash argues that a `best point' $\vv{x}^*$ should have the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
	\item $x^*_j \geq s_j$ for all $j$: nobody is worse off than if they had walked away.
	\item (\emph{Pareto optimality}) If $\vv{x} \in E$ and $x_j \geq x_j^*$ for all $j$, then $\vv{x}=\vv{x}^*$: there is no available outcome which makes somebody better off and nobody worse off.
	\item (\emph{Independence of irrelevant alternatives}) If $E' \supseteq E$ is closed, bounded and convex and $\vv{x}^{**}$ is the best point for $(E',\vv{s})$, then $\vv{x}^{**}\in E$ implies that $\vv{x}^{**}$ is the best point for $(E,\vv{s})$.
	\item (\emph{Symmetry}) If $E$ is invariant under permutation of coordinates and $\vv{s}$ is too, then $x_1^*=x_2^*=\cdots=x_m^*$.
	\item (\emph{Scale invariance}) If $T\vv{x} = (a_1x_1+b_1,\ldots,a_mx_m+b_m)$ with $a_j>0$, and $\vv{x}^*$ is the best point for $(E,\vv{s})$, then $T\vv{x}^*$ is the best point for $(TE, T\vv{s})$.
\end{enumerate}
Condition (5) says that we must treat the poor man's penny with the same respect as the rich man's pound: nothing should depend on the units in which each participant measures their satisfaction. It is a genuine assumption, but a reasonable one, and it is the one which does most of the work.

There is no difficulty in remembering these conditions, since each plays a specific role in the proof. We begin with a lemma which is a very concrete instance of the separating hyperplane theorem.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nash-hyperplane}
	Let $K$ be a convex subset of $\R^n$ with $(1,1,\ldots,1)\in K$ and $\prod_{j=1}^n x_j \leq 1$ for all $\vv{x} \in K$ with every $x_j \geq 0$. Then
	$$K \subseteq \Big\{\vv{x} : \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^n x_j \leq n\Big\}.$$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Let $\vv{x}\in K$ and let $\vv{1} = (1,\ldots,1)$. By convexity $\delta\vv{x} + (1-\delta)\vv{1} \in K$ for $0\leq\delta\leq1$, and for $\delta$ small enough every coordinate $1 + \delta(x_j-1)$ is positive. So the hypothesis applies and
	$$\prod_{j=1}^n \big(1+\delta(x_j-1)\big) \leq 1.$$
	Expanding, the left-hand side equals $1 + \delta\sum_{j=1}^n (x_j-1) + \delta^2 R(\delta)$ where $R(\delta)$ stays bounded as $\delta \to 0$. Hence $\sum_{j=1}^n (x_j-1) + \delta R(\delta) \leq 0$, and letting $\delta \to 0^+$ gives $\sum_j x_j \leq n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nash-max}
	Let $E \subseteq\R^m$ be closed, bounded and convex with $\vv{s} \in \Int E$. Then the function $f(\vv{x}) = \prod_{j=1}^m (x_j - s_j)$ attains a maximum on $E^+ = \{\vv{x} \in E : x_j\geq s_j \text{ for all } j\}$ at exactly one point.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	The set $E^+$ is closed, bounded and non-empty, so $f$ attains a maximum $M$ there. Since $\vv{s} \in \Int E$ we can find $\vv{x} \in E$ with $x_j > s_j$ for all $j$, so $M>0$.

	For uniqueness, suppose $f(\vv{x}) = f(\vv{y}) = M$ with $\vv{x},\vv{y}\in E^+$. Let $\vv{z} = \frac12(\vv{x}+\vv{y}) \in E^+$ by convexity. By the AM--GM inequality,
	$$z_j - s_j = \frac{(x_j-s_j)+(y_j-s_j)}{2} \geq \sqrt{(x_j-s_j)(y_j-s_j)},$$
	with equality if and only if $x_j = y_j$. Multiplying over $j$ (all factors are non-negative) gives $f(\vv{z}) \geq \sqrt{f(\vv{x})f(\vv{y})} = M$. As $M$ is the maximum, we have equality throughout, and since $M>0$ every factor is strictly positive, so equality in AM--GM forces $x_j = y_j$ for every $j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Nash Bargaining]\label{thm:nash-bargain}
	Suppose that to each problem $(E,\vv{s})$ (with $E \subseteq \R^m$ closed, bounded and convex and $\vv{s}\in\Int E$) our conditions assign a best point. Then the best point of $(E,\vv{s})$ is the unique maximiser of
	$$f(\vv{x}) = \prod_{j=1}^m (x_j-s_j)$$
	over $\{\vv{x}\in E : x_j \geq s_j\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Let $\vv{x}^*$ be the maximiser given by Lemma \ref{lem:nash-max}, so $x^*_j > s_j$ for every $j$. By condition (5) we may apply the affine change of coordinates $x_j \mapsto (x_j - s_j)/(x_j^*-s_j)$; this replaces $\vv{s}$ by $\vv{0}$ and $\vv{x}^*$ by $\vv{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)$, and does not change which point is `best'. So assume $\vv{s} = \vv{0}$ and $\vv{x}^*=\vv{1}$.

	Maximality of $f$ now reads: $\prod_j x_j \leq 1$ for every $\vv{x} \in E$ with all $x_j \geq 0$. As $E$ is convex and contains $\vv{1}$, Lemma \ref{lem:nash-hyperplane} gives $E \subseteq \{\vv{x} : \sum_j x_j \leq m\}$. Pick $M$ so large that $E \subseteq [-M,M]^m$ and set
	$$\Sigma = \Big\{\vv{x} \in [-M,M]^m : \textstyle\sum_j x_j \leq m\Big\},$$
	which is closed, bounded, convex, contains $E$, contains $\vv{0}$ in its interior, and is invariant under permutations of the coordinates.

	Consider the problem $(\Sigma,\vv{0})$. By symmetry (condition (4)) its best point has all coordinates equal, say $\vv{y} = (t,t,\ldots,t)$ with $mt\leq m$, so $t\leq 1$. By Pareto optimality (condition (2)), since $\vv{1}\in\Sigma$ dominates $\vv{y}$ coordinatewise when $t<1$, we must have $t=1$; that is, the best point of $(\Sigma,\vv{0})$ is $\vv{1} = \vv{x}^*$. Finally $\vv{x}^*\in E \subseteq \Sigma$, so by independence of irrelevant alternatives (condition (3)) $\vv{x}^*$ is the best point of $(E,\vv{0})$.
\end{proof}

So the fair division is the one which maximises the \emph{product} of the gains over the status quo -- a rule with no obvious a priori justification which nonetheless falls out of five innocuous-looking requirements. There is no patent for immortality under the moon, but I suspect Nash's results will be remembered long after the last celluloid copy of \emph{A Beautiful Mind} has crumbled to dust.

\section{Approximation by Polynomials}

It is a guiding idea of both the calculus and of numerical analysis that well behaved functions look like polynomials. Like most guiding principles, it needs to be used judiciously, and this section is devoted to finding out in what sense it is actually true.

If asked to justify the principle we might mutter something about Taylor's theorem. But Cauchy produced the following example to show that this is not enough.

\begin{example}[Cauchy's Example]\label{ex:cauchy}
	Define $E : \R\to\R$ by
	$$E(t) = \begin{cases} \exp(-1/t^2) & t\neq 0,\\ 0 & t=0.\end{cases}$$
	Then $E$ is infinitely differentiable, and $E^{(n)}(0)=0$ for every $n$, yet $E(t)\neq 0$ for $t \neq 0$. So the Taylor series of $E$ about $0$ converges everywhere, to the wrong function.

	Indeed, for $t\neq 0$ an easy induction gives $E^{(n)}(t) = P_n(1/t)E(t)$ for some polynomial $P_n$: differentiating $P_n(1/t)E(t)$ gives $\left(-t^{-2}P_n'(1/t) + 2t^{-3}P_n(1/t)\right)E(t)$, which is of the same shape. At $0$ we induct again: if $E^{(n)}(0)=0$ then
	$$\frac{E^{(n)}(h)-E^{(n)}(0)}{h} = \frac1h P_n(1/h)\exp(-1/h^2) \to 0$$
	as $h\to 0$, since $x^k e^{-x^2}\to 0$ as $|x|\to\infty$ for every $k$. So $E^{(n+1)}(0)$ exists and is $0$.
\end{example}

By Part II this example has been promoted from a counterexample to an invaluable tool: it is what makes partitions of unity and smoothing kernels possible. But it does show that Taylor's theorem is not going to tell us that continuous functions look like polynomials.

\subsection{Interpolation and Chebyshev Polynomials}

We might instead mutter something about interpolation. Write $\Pn_n$ for the vector space of real polynomials of degree at most $n$.

\begin{lemma}[Lagrange Interpolation]\label{lem:lagrange}
	Let $x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_n$ be distinct points of $[a,b]$ and let $f : [a,b] \to\R$.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item There is at most one $P \in \Pn_n$ with $P(x_j) = f(x_j)$ for $0\leq j\leq n$.
		\item Writing $e_j(t) = \prod_{k\neq j}\frac{t-x_k}{x_j-x_k}$, the polynomial $P = \sum_{j=0}^n f(x_j)e_j$ lies in $\Pn_n$ and satisfies $P(x_i)=f(x_i)$ for all $i$.
		\item The $e_j$ form a basis of $\Pn_n$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	(i) The difference of two such polynomials lies in $\Pn_n$ and vanishes at $n+1$ points, so is zero by Lemma \ref{lem:poly-roots}. (ii) Each $e_j$ has degree $n$, and $e_j(x_i)=\delta_{ij}$ by construction, so $P(x_i) = f(x_i)$. (iii) There are $n+1$ of them and $\dim \Pn_n = n+1$; they span by (ii).
\end{proof}

So there is always a unique interpolating polynomial. Unfortunately, `interpolates $f$ at $n+1$ points' is a long way from `is close to $f$'. To see how badly things can go we introduce a family of polynomials which will be with us for the rest of the course.

\begin{theorem}[Chebyshev Polynomials]\label{thm:chebyshev}
	There are polynomials $T_n$ of degree $n$ and $U_{n-1}$ of degree $n-1$ such that
	$$T_n(\cos\theta) = \cos n\theta, \qquad U_{n-1}(\cos\theta) = \frac{\sin n\theta}{\sin\theta}$$
	for all $\theta$ (the second whenever $\sin\theta \neq 0$). The coefficient of $t^n$ in $T_n$ is $2^{n-1}$ for $n\geq 1$. The roots of $T_n$ are $\cos\left(\frac{(2r+1)\pi}{2n}\right)$ for $0\leq r\leq n-1$, and the roots of $U_{n-1}$ are $\cos(r\pi/n)$ for $1\leq r\leq n-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	By de Moivre's theorem and the binomial expansion,
	\begin{align*}
		\cos n\theta + i \sin n\theta &= (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n \\
		&= \sum_{0\leq 2r\leq n}(-1)^r\binom{n}{2r}\cos^{n-2r}\theta\,\sin^{2r}\theta \\
		&\qquad + i\sum_{0\leq 2r+1\leq n}(-1)^r\binom{n}{2r+1}\cos^{n-2r-1}\theta\,\sin^{2r}\theta\cdot\sin\theta.
	\end{align*}
	Now $\sin^{2r}\theta = (1-\cos^2\theta)^r$, so comparing real and imaginary parts we get $\cos n\theta = T_n(\cos\theta)$ and $\sin n\theta = \sin\theta\, U_{n-1}(\cos\theta)$ where
	$$T_n(t) = \sum_{0\leq 2r\leq n}(-1)^r\binom{n}{2r}t^{n-2r}(1-t^2)^r, \quad U_{n-1}(t) = \sum_{0\leq 2r+1\leq n}(-1)^r\binom{n}{2r+1}t^{n-2r-1}(1-t^2)^r.$$
	These are polynomials of degrees $n$ and $n-1$. The coefficient of $t^n$ in $T_n$ comes from taking the top power in each term, giving
	$$\sum_{0\leq 2r\leq n}(-1)^r\binom{n}{2r}(-1)^r = \sum_{0\leq 2r\leq n}\binom{n}{2r} = \frac{(1+1)^n+(1-1)^n}{2} = 2^{n-1}$$
	for $n \geq 1$. Finally $T_n(\cos\theta)=0$ exactly when $n\theta$ is an odd multiple of $\pi/2$, and as $\theta$ ranges over $[0,\pi]$ this gives the $n$ distinct roots stated; the argument for $U_{n-1}$ is the same.
\end{proof}

The picture already shows the two features of the $T_n$ which will matter to us: on $[-1,1]$ they oscillate between $+1$ and $-1$, hitting each extreme value alternately and as often as possible, and they are as large as a monic-up-to-scale polynomial can be forced to be while doing so.

Now look at $U_{n-1}$. Its roots are the $n-1$ points $\cos(r\pi/n)$, which together with $\pm1$ are perfectly reasonably spaced points of $[-1,1]$; but $U_{n-1}(1) = n$, which is large. So the function $U_{n-1}$ is `well behaved' at $n+1$ well spread points (vanishing at $n-1$ of them) and yet is very large elsewhere. Interpolation at innocent-looking points can be badly misleading, and it can be shown -- though this is harder -- that no choice of interpolation points avoids this in general.

The classical illustration is due to Runge. Take $f(x) = (1+25x^2)^{-1}$ on $[-1,1]$, a function about as well behaved as one could wish, and interpolate it at $11$ equally spaced points. This is a warning about interpolation, not about polynomials. The question we actually want to answer is whether \emph{some} polynomial is uniformly close to $f$, and to that we now turn.

\subsection{Which Metric?}

Before we can ask whether $f$ looks like $g$, we must say what we mean. There are several natural metrics on $C([a,b])$, and they are genuinely different.

\begin{example}[Three Norms]
	On $C([0,1])$ the formulae
	$$\norm{f}_1 = \int_0^1|f(t)|\dd t, \qquad \norm{f}_2 = \left(\int_0^1 f(t)^2\dd t\right)^{1/2}, \qquad \norm{f}_\infty = \sup_{t\in[0,1]}|f(t)|$$
	all define norms, and hence metrics $d_k(f,g) = \norm{f-g}_k$. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality $\norm{f}_1 \leq \norm{f}_2$, and clearly $\norm{f}_2\leq\norm{f}_\infty$, so
	$$\norm{f}_\infty \geq \norm{f}_2\geq \norm{f}_1.$$
	These inequalities go only one way. If $f_n(t) = \max\{1-nt,0\}$ then $\norm{f_n}_\infty = 1$, $\norm{f_n}_2 = (3n)^{-1/2}$ and $\norm{f_n}_1 = (2n)^{-1}$, so $\norm{f_n}_\infty/\norm{f_n}_1 = 2n \to \infty$. A tall thin spike is large in $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$ and negligible in $\norm{\cdot}_1$.
\end{example}

All three are used in practice: $\norm{\cdot}_2$ is the natural one when there is an inner product around (as in least squares fitting, and in Fourier analysis), while $\norm{\cdot}_1$ is the robust one favoured by statisticians. We shall work almost exclusively with $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$, because it is the one for which `looks like' means what an engineer would want it to mean, and because of the following fact from IB Analysis.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cab-complete}
	The space $C([a,b])$ with the uniform metric $d(f,g) = \norm{f-g}_\infty$ is complete.
\end{theorem}

This is the general principle of uniform convergence: a uniformly Cauchy sequence of continuous functions converges uniformly, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous.

\subsection{The Weierstrass Approximation Theorem}

We can now state the question precisely, and answer it. Weierstrass proved the following in a paper published when he was seventy.

\begin{theorem}[Weierstrass Approximation Theorem]\label{thm:weierstrass}
	Let $f \in C([a,b])$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Then there is a polynomial $P$ with $\norm{f-P}_\infty<\varepsilon$.
\end{theorem}

Several proofs are known, and it is my belief that one cannot have too many insightful proofs of Weierstrass's theorem. We give one due to Bernstein, which is based on probability and which has the great virtue of writing down an explicit approximating polynomial. We need one tool from IA Probability.

\begin{theorem}[Chebyshev's Inequality]\label{thm:chebyshev-ineq}
	If $X$ is a bounded real random variable with $\sigma^2 = \var X$, then $\Pr(|X-\EE X|\geq a) \leq \sigma^2/a^2$ for all $a>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Writing $\mu = \EE X$ we have $\sigma^2 = \EE(X-\mu)^2 \geq \EE\left((X-\mu)^2\ii_{|X-\mu|\geq a}\right) \geq a^2\Pr(|X-\mu|\geq a)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Bernstein]\label{thm:bernstein}
	Let $f\in C([0,1])$ and define the \vocab{Bernstein polynomial}
	$$p_n(t) = \sum_{r=0}^n\binom{n}{r}f\!\left(\frac{r}{n}\right)t^r(1-t)^{n-r}.$$
	Then $p_n \in \Pn_n$ and $\norm{p_n - f}_\infty\to 0$ as $n \to\infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	The probabilistic idea is this. Fix $t\in[0,1]$ and let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be independent with $\Pr(X_r=1)=t$ and $\Pr(X_r=0)=1-t$ -- that is, toss a coin with bias $t$. Put $Y_n = (X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$. Then $nY_n$ is binomial, so
	$$\EE f(Y_n) = \sum_{r=0}^n \Pr\!\left(Y_n = \frac rn\right)f\!\left(\frac rn\right) = \sum_{r=0}^n\binom nr t^r(1-t)^{n-r}f\!\left(\frac rn\right) = p_n(t).$$
	In other words, $p_n(t)$ is the average of $f$ over a distribution concentrated near $t$. Since $\EE Y_n = t$ and $\var Y_n = t(1-t)/n \leq 1/(4n)$, the concentration improves as $n$ grows, and continuity of $f$ should do the rest.

	Let us do this carefully. Let $\varepsilon>0$. As $[0,1]$ is compact, $f$ is uniformly continuous (Theorem \ref{thm:unif-cts}), so there is a $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|x-y|<\delta$. Also $\norm{f}_\infty<\infty$. Splitting according to whether $Y_n$ is close to $t$,
	\begin{align*}
		|p_n(t)-f(t)| &= \left|\EE\big(f(Y_n)-f(t)\big)\right| \\
		&\leq \EE\left(\ii_{|Y_n-t|<\delta}\,|f(Y_n)-f(t)|\right) + \EE\left(\ii_{|Y_n-t|\geq\delta}\,|f(Y_n)-f(t)|\right)\\
		&\leq \varepsilon + 2\norm{f}_\infty\Pr(|Y_n-t|\geq\delta) \\
		&\leq \varepsilon + 2\norm{f}_\infty\frac{\var Y_n}{\delta^2} \leq \varepsilon + \frac{\norm{f}_\infty}{2n\delta^2},
	\end{align*}
	using Chebyshev's inequality. The final bound does not depend on $t$, so
	$$\norm{p_n-f}_\infty \leq \varepsilon + \frac{\norm{f}_\infty}{2n\delta^2} \leq 2\varepsilon$$
	for all $n$ large enough. As $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\norm{p_n-f}_\infty\to0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:weierstrass}]
	Let $f\in C([a,b])$ and set $g(t) = f(a + (b-a)t)$, so $g \in C([0,1])$. By Theorem \ref{thm:bernstein} there is a polynomial $q$ with $\norm{g-q}_\infty<\varepsilon$, and then $P(x) = q\left(\frac{x-a}{b-a}\right)$ is a polynomial with $\norm{f-P}_\infty<\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\section{Best Approximation by Polynomials}

Bernstein's theorem gives us an explicit approximating polynomial, but except in very special circumstances it is not the \emph{best} one -- indeed we have not yet even shown that a best one exists. Chebyshev was very interested in this question, and gave a beautiful criterion for recognising a best approximation when you see one.

The idea is easy to describe. If $P$ is a good approximation to $f$ then the error $f - P$ should not be lopsided: if it were positive more than negative, we could shift $P$ up a little and do better. Chebyshev's insight is that the error of the \emph{best} approximation must swing between its extreme values as often as it possibly can.

\begin{theorem}[Chebyshev Equiripple Criterion]\label{thm:equiripple}
	Let $f\in C([a,b])$ and $P \in \Pn_{n-1}$, and write $\sigma = \norm{f-P}_\infty$. Suppose we can find points $a \leq a_0 < a_1 < \cdots < a_n\leq b$ and a sign $\zeta\in\{-1,+1\}$ such that
	$$f(a_j)-P(a_j) = \zeta(-1)^j\sigma \qquad (0\leq j\leq n).$$
	Then $\norm{f-P}_\infty \leq \norm{f-Q}_\infty$ for every $Q \in \Pn_{n-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Replacing $f$ and $P$ by $-f$ and $-P$ if necessary, we may assume $\zeta = 1$, so $f(a_j)-P(a_j) = (-1)^j\sigma$. Suppose for contradiction that some $Q\in\Pn_{n-1}$ has $\norm{f-Q}_\infty < \sigma$, and set $R = P - Q\in\Pn_{n-1}$. Then
	$$R(a_j) = \big(f(a_j)-Q(a_j)\big) - \big(f(a_j)-P(a_j)\big) = \big(f(a_j)-Q(a_j)\big) - (-1)^j\sigma.$$
	Since $|f(a_j)-Q(a_j)|\leq \norm{f-Q}_\infty<\sigma$, the sign of $R(a_j)$ is that of $-(-1)^j$, and in particular $R(a_j)\neq 0$. So $R$ takes alternate signs at the $n+1$ points $a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_n$, and by the intermediate value theorem $R$ has a root in each of the $n$ intervals $(a_j,a_{j+1})$. Thus $R$ is a non-zero element of $\Pn_{n-1}$ with at least $n$ distinct roots, which is impossible.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	The equiripple condition is also \emph{necessary}: the best approximation always equioscillates in this way, and in particular it is unique. The proof is a pleasant exercise but is not part of the course.
\end{remark}

Chebyshev's criterion is designed to be used in one direction: guess a candidate, check that its error equioscillates, and conclude. Here is the classic application, and it explains where the Chebyshev polynomials really come from.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:best-approx-tn}
	Work on $[-1,1]$ and let $n\geq1$. Write $S_n(t) = t^n - 2^{1-n}T_n(t)$, which lies in $\Pn_{n-1}$ by Theorem \ref{thm:chebyshev}. Then
	$$\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}|t^n - Q(t)| \geq \sup_{t\in[-1,1]}|t^n-S_n(t)| = 2^{1-n}$$
	for every $Q\in\Pn_{n-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	By construction $t^n - S_n(t) = 2^{1-n}T_n(t)$, and $|T_n(\cos\theta)| = |\cos n\theta|\leq1$ with $\norm{T_n}_\infty=1$ on $[-1,1]$, so $\sigma := \norm{t^n - S_n}_\infty = 2^{1-n}$. Let $a_j = \cos\left(\frac{(n-j)\pi}{n}\right)$ for $0\leq j \leq n$, so that $-1 = a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_n = 1$ and
	$$a_j^n - S_n(a_j) = 2^{1-n}\cos\big((n-j)\pi\big) = (-1)^{n-j}\sigma = (-1)^n(-1)^j\sigma.$$
	So the equiripple criterion of Theorem \ref{thm:equiripple} applies with $\zeta = (-1)^n$, and $S_n$ is a best approximation to $t^n$ from $\Pn_{n-1}$.
\end{proof}

So the Chebyshev polynomial $2^{1-n}T_n$ is precisely the monic polynomial of degree $n$ with smallest uniform norm on $[-1,1]$. This immediately gives useful lower bounds on the size of polynomials with large coefficients.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:coeff-bound}
	We work on $[-1,1]$.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item If $P(t) = \sum_{j=0}^n a_jt^j$ with $|a_n|\geq1$, then $\norm{P}_\infty \geq 2^{1-n}$.
		\item There is an $\varepsilon(n)>0$ with the following property: if $P(t) = \sum_{j=0}^n a_jt^j$ and $|a_k|\geq1$ for \emph{some} $0\leq k\leq n$, then $\norm{P}_\infty\geq \varepsilon(n)$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
	(i) Write $P/a_n = t^n - Q(t)$ with $Q \in \Pn_{n-1}$. By Theorem \ref{thm:best-approx-tn}, $\norm{P}_\infty/|a_n| \geq 2^{1-n}$, and $|a_n|\geq1$.

	(ii) We induct on $n$. For $n = 0$ we may take $\varepsilon(0)=1$. Suppose $\varepsilon(n)$ works, and let $P(t) = \sum_{j=0}^{n+1}a_jt^j$ with $|a_k|\geq1$ for some $k$. If $|a_{n+1}|\geq \varepsilon(n)/2$ then (i) applied to $P/a_{n+1}$ gives $\norm{P}_\infty\geq|a_{n+1}|2^{-n}\geq\varepsilon(n)2^{-n-1}$. If instead $|a_{n+1}|<\varepsilon(n)/2$ then $k\leq n$, so writing $R(t) = \sum_{j=0}^n a_jt^j$ we have $\norm{R}_\infty\geq\varepsilon(n)$ by the inductive hypothesis, and
	$$\norm{P}_\infty \geq \norm{R}_\infty - |a_{n+1}|\norm{t^{n+1}}_\infty \geq \varepsilon(n)-\frac{\varepsilon(n)}{2} = \frac{\varepsilon(n)}{2}.$$
	So $\varepsilon(n+1) = \varepsilon(n)2^{-n-1}$ will do.
\end{proof}

What part (ii) really says is that on the finite dimensional space $\Pn_n$ the uniform norm and the norm $\max_j|a_j|$ on coefficients are equivalent. That is exactly what we need to run a compactness argument.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:best-exists}
	If $f\in C([a,b])$ and $n\geq0$, then there is a $P\in\Pn_n$ with $\norm{f-P}_\infty\leq\norm{f-Q}_\infty$ for all $Q\in\Pn_n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	A linear change of variable turns $[a,b]$ into $[-1,1]$ and carries $\Pn_n$ bijectively onto itself, so we may assume $[a,b]=[-1,1]$. Define $\Phi : \R^{n+1}\to\R$ by
	$$\Phi(a_0,\ldots,a_n) = \Big\lVert f - \sum_{j=0}^n a_jt^j\Big\rVert_\infty.$$
	Then $\Phi$ is continuous: indeed $|\Phi(\vv{a})-\Phi(\vv{b})| \leq \norm{\sum(a_j-b_j)t^j}_\infty \leq \sum_j|a_j-b_j|$.

	Let $\kappa = \inf\Phi$, and note $\kappa\leq\Phi(\vv{0}) = \norm{f}_\infty$. If $\Phi(\vv{a})\leq\norm{f}_\infty$ then $\norm{\sum a_jt^j}_\infty \leq 2\norm f_\infty$, and so, writing $M = \max_j|a_j|$, Corollary \ref{cor:coeff-bound}(ii) applied to $M^{-1}\sum a_jt^j$ (which has a coefficient of modulus $1$) gives $\norm{\sum a_jt^j}_\infty\geq M\varepsilon(n)$ whenever $M > 0$. Hence
	$$M \leq \frac{2\norm f_\infty}{\varepsilon(n)}.$$
	So the set $E = \{\vv{a} : \Phi(\vv{a})\leq\norm f_\infty\}$ is bounded, and it is closed since $\Phi$ is continuous. Thus $E$ is compact and non-empty, so $\Phi$ attains a minimum on $E$; since $\Phi \geq \Phi(\vv{0})$ off $E$, this is a global minimum.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Least Squares versus Uniform Approximation]
	It is worth contrasting this with best approximation in the $\norm{\cdot}_2$ norm. There, $\Pn_n$ is a finite dimensional subspace of an inner product space, and the best approximation is simply the orthogonal projection of $f$ onto $\Pn_n$: it exists, is unique, is characterised by $\langle f - P, Q\rangle = 0$ for all $Q\in\Pn_n$, and can be computed by expanding $f$ in an orthonormal basis. All of that is linear algebra. The uniform norm has no inner product behind it, which is why we have had to work for existence, and why the characterisation (equioscillation) is a genuinely analytic statement.
\end{remark}

\section{Gaussian Quadrature}

Suppose we want to estimate $\int_a^b f(x)\dd x$ knowing only the values of $f$ at finitely many points. The naive approach is to interpolate and integrate the interpolant, which leads at once to the following.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:quad-weights}
	Let $x_0,\ldots,x_n$ be distinct points of $[a,b]$. Then there are unique real numbers $A_0,\ldots,A_n$ with
	$$\int_a^b P(x)\dd x = \sum_{j=0}^n A_jP(x_j)$$
	for all $P \in\Pn_n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	With $e_j$ as in Lemma \ref{lem:lagrange}, every $P\in\Pn_n$ satisfies $P = \sum_j P(x_j)e_j$, so setting $A_j = \int_a^b e_j(x)\dd x$ and integrating gives the formula. Conversely, if the formula holds then putting $P = e_i$ gives $\int_a^b e_i = \sum_j A_j e_i(x_j) = A_i$, so the $A_j$ are determined.
\end{proof}

Given the previous section's warnings about interpolation, we should be suspicious of the resulting approximation $\int_a^b f \approx \sum_j A_jf(x_j)$. Our suspicion is justified: when the $x_j$ are equally spaced (the Newton--Cotes rules), computation shows that as $n$ grows the $A_j$ start to vary in sign and $\sum_j|A_j|$ grows rapidly. Since an error of size $\eta$ in the values $f(x_j)$ produces an error of up to $\eta\sum_j|A_j|$ in the answer, the scheme becomes worse as we use more points.

It is a genuinely surprising fact that a clever choice of points avoids this completely. The clever choice is due to Gauss, and involves the orthogonal polynomials for the inner product
$$\langle f,g\rangle = \int_{-1}^1 f(t)g(t)\dd t$$
on $C([-1,1])$.

\begin{definition}[Legendre Polynomials]
	The \vocab{Legendre polynomials} $p_0,p_1,p_2,\ldots$ are defined by the conditions that $p_n$ has degree exactly $n$ with positive leading coefficient, and $\langle p_n,p_m\rangle = \delta_{nm}$.
\end{definition}

They exist and are unique: apply Gram--Schmidt to $1,t,t^2,\ldots$, so that
$$q_n(t) = t^n - \sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\langle t^n,p_m\rangle p_m(t), \qquad p_n = \frac{q_n}{\norm{q_n}_2}.$$
Here $q_n\neq0$ because it has leading coefficient $1$. By construction $p_0,\ldots,p_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $\Pn_n$, and $p_n\perp \Pn_{n-1}$. (Other normalisations of the Legendre polynomials are common; they differ from ours only by a positive scalar.)

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:legendre-roots}
	The polynomial $p_n$ has $n$ distinct roots, all lying in $(-1,1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Let $y_1,\ldots,y_m$ be the distinct points of $(-1,1)$ at which $p_n$ changes sign, and set $Q(t) = \prod_{j=1}^m(t-y_j)$ (with $Q=1$ if $m=0$). Then $p_nQ$ does not change sign anywhere on $[-1,1]$, and is not identically zero, so
	$$\langle p_n, Q\rangle = \int_{-1}^1 p_n(t)Q(t)\dd t \neq 0.$$
	But $p_n\perp\Pn_{n-1}$, so we cannot have $\deg Q = m \leq n-1$. Hence $m \geq n$; as $p_n$ has degree $n$ it has at most $n$ roots, so $m=n$, the roots are simple and all lie in $(-1,1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Gaussian Quadrature]\label{thm:gauss-quad}
	Let $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$ be the roots of $p_n$ and let $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ be the weights of Lemma \ref{lem:quad-weights} for these points, so that $\int_{-1}^1 P = \sum_j A_jP(\alpha_j)$ for all $P \in \Pn_{n-1}$.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item In fact $\int_{-1}^1 Q(x)\dd x = \sum_{j=1}^nA_jQ(\alpha_j)$ for every $Q\in\Pn_{2n-1}$.
		\item Conversely, if $\beta_j\in[-1,1]$ and $B_j$ are such that $\int_{-1}^1 Q = \sum_j B_jQ(\beta_j)$ for all $Q\in\Pn_{2n-1}$, then the $\beta_j$ are the roots of $p_n$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	(i) Let $Q\in\Pn_{2n-1}$. Dividing by $p_n$ we may write $Q = Gp_n + R$ with $G,R\in\Pn_{n-1}$. Then
	$$\int_{-1}^1 Q = \int_{-1}^1 Gp_n + \int_{-1}^1 R = \langle G,p_n\rangle + \sum_{j=1}^n A_jR(\alpha_j) = 0 + \sum_{j=1}^n A_jR(\alpha_j),$$
	using $p_n\perp\Pn_{n-1}$ and the defining property of the $A_j$. But $p_n(\alpha_j)=0$, so $R(\alpha_j) = Q(\alpha_j)$, giving the result.

	(ii) Set $W(x) = \prod_{j=1}^n(x-\beta_j)$, a monic polynomial of degree $n$. For any $R\in\Pn_{n-1}$ the product $WR$ lies in $\Pn_{2n-1}$ and vanishes at every $\beta_j$, so
	$$\langle W,R\rangle = \int_{-1}^1 W R = \sum_{j=1}^n B_jW(\beta_j)R(\beta_j) = 0.$$
	Hence $W \in\Pn_n$ is orthogonal to $\Pn_{n-1}$. Since $p_0,\ldots,p_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $\Pn_n$ and $p_0,\ldots,p_{n-1}$ spans $\Pn_{n-1}$, the orthogonal complement of $\Pn_{n-1}$ in $\Pn_n$ is one-dimensional and spanned by $p_n$. So $W$ is a scalar multiple of $p_n$, and the $\beta_j$ are the roots of $p_n$.
\end{proof}

Doubling the degree of exactness for free is impressive, but the real payoff is the following, which shows that the objection to Newton--Cotes evaporates.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:gauss-good}
	With the notation of Theorem \ref{thm:gauss-quad}, write $G_nf = \sum_{j=1}^nA_jf(\alpha_j)$.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item $A_j > 0$ for each $j$.
		\item $\sum_{j=1}^n A_j = 2$.
		\item If $f \in C([-1,1])$ and $P\in\Pn_{2n-1}$, then $\left|\int_{-1}^1 f - G_nf\right| \leq 4\norm{f-P}_\infty$.
		\item If $f\in C([-1,1])$ then $G_nf \to \int_{-1}^1 f(t)\dd t$ as $n\to\infty$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	(i) Fix $k$ and let $w_k(t) = \prod_{j\neq k}(t-\alpha_j)^2$, a polynomial of degree $2n-2 \leq 2n-1$. By Theorem \ref{thm:gauss-quad}(i),
	$$0 < \int_{-1}^1 w_k(t)\dd t = \sum_{j=1}^n A_jw_k(\alpha_j) = A_kw_k(\alpha_k),$$
	since $w_k$ vanishes at every $\alpha_j$ with $j \neq k$. As $w_k(\alpha_k)>0$ we get $A_k>0$.

	(ii) Take $Q = 1$ in Theorem \ref{thm:gauss-quad}(i).

	(iii) Since $\int_{-1}^1 P = G_nP$,
	\begin{align*}
		\left|\int_{-1}^1 f - G_nf\right| &= \left|\int_{-1}^1(f-P) - \sum_j A_j\big(f(\alpha_j)-P(\alpha_j)\big)\right|\\
		&\leq \int_{-1}^1|f-P| + \sum_j |A_j|\,|f(\alpha_j)-P(\alpha_j)|\\
		&\leq 2\norm{f-P}_\infty + \left(\sum_j A_j\right)\norm{f-P}_\infty = 4\norm{f-P}_\infty,
	\end{align*}
	where we used (i) to drop the modulus signs on the $A_j$ and (ii) to evaluate their sum.

	(iv) Given $\varepsilon>0$, Weierstrass's Theorem \ref{thm:weierstrass} provides a polynomial $P$ with $\norm{f-P}_\infty<\varepsilon/4$. For all $n$ with $2n-1\geq\deg P$, part (iii) gives $\left|\int_{-1}^1f - G_nf\right| < \varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

It is worth appreciating how the pieces fit together. Positivity of the weights (i) is what stops the errors from amplifying; it is a consequence of the choice of nodes, and it is exactly what fails for equally spaced nodes. Once we have positivity and (ii), the quadrature error is controlled by the best polynomial approximation error, and Weierstrass's theorem then guarantees convergence for \emph{every} continuous $f$ -- with no smoothness assumptions at all.

\section{Distance and Compact Sets}

This section is a digression, but a useful one: we construct a metric on the collection of compact sets. It provides some helpful background for Runge's theorem, and it will supply a striking application of the Baire category theorem later on.

Throughout we work in $\R^m$ with the usual metric, and all sets are non-empty and compact. Recall from Lemma \ref{lem:dist-cts} that $d(\vv{x},A) = \inf_{\vv{a}\in A}\norm{\vv{x}-\vv{a}}$ defines a continuous function of $\vv{x}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nearest-point}
	Let $E$ be a non-empty compact subset of $\R^m$ and $\vv{a}\in\R^m$. Then there is a point $\vv{e}\in E$ with $\norm{\vv{a}-\vv{e}} = d(\vv{a},E)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	The map $\vv{x}\mapsto \norm{\vv{a}-\vv{x}}$ is continuous on the compact set $E$, so attains its infimum by Theorem \ref{thm:attains-bounds}.
\end{proof}

The nearest point need not be unique -- take $E$ to be a circle and $\vv{a}$ its centre -- but it is unique if $E$ is convex. Indeed if $\vv{u},\vv{v}\in E$ both achieve the minimum distance $\rho$ from $\vv{a}$ and $\vv u\neq\vv v$, then $\tfrac12(\vv u+\vv v) \in E$ and, by the parallelogram law,
$$\Big\lVert\vv a - \tfrac{\vv u + \vv v}{2}\Big\rVert^2 = \rho^2 - \tfrac14\norm{\vv u - \vv v}^2 < \rho^2,$$
a contradiction.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:two-sets-min}
	Let $E,F\subseteq\R^m$ be non-empty.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item If $E$ and $F$ are compact, there exist $\vv e\in E$, $\vv f\in F$ with $\norm{\vv e-\vv f}\leq\norm{\vv x - \vv y}$ for all $\vv x\in E$, $\vv y\in F$.
		\item The same holds if $E$ is compact and $F$ is closed.
		\item It may fail if $E$ and $F$ are merely closed.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	(i) The function $(\vv x,\vv y)\mapsto \norm{\vv x-\vv y}$ is continuous on the compact set $E\times F$.

	(ii) Choose $R>0$ with $E \subseteq \overline{B}(\vv 0,R)$ and $F \cap \overline{B}(\vv 0,R)\neq\emptyset$. Set $F_R = F\cap\overline{B}(\vv 0,3R)$, a non-empty compact set. By (i) there are $\vv e\in E$, $\vv f\in F_R$ minimising the distance over $E\times F_R$, and $\norm{\vv e - \vv f}\leq 2R$. If $\vv y \in F\setminus F_R$ then $\norm{\vv x-\vv y}\geq 3R - R = 2R$ for $\vv x \in E$, so the minimum over $E\times F$ is achieved too.

	(iii) Take $E = \{(x,1/x):x>0\}$ and $F = \{(-x,1/x):x>0\}$. Both are closed, $\norm{\vv x -\vv y}>0$ for all $\vv x\in E$, $\vv y \in F$, but taking $x$ large shows the infimum is $0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Hausdorff Metric}

We would like a notion of distance between compact sets. The first thing one writes down is
$$\tau(E,F) = \inf_{\vv e\in E}d(\vv e,F) = \inf\{\norm{\vv e-\vv f} : \vv e\in E,\ \vv f\in F\}.$$
This is symmetric and non-negative, and by Lemma \ref{lem:two-sets-min} we have $\tau(E,F)=0$ if and only if $E\cap F\neq\emptyset$. So it fails the axiom $\tau(E,F)=0 \Rightarrow E=F$ rather badly, and it also fails the triangle inequality: with $A = \{1\}$, $B = \{1,2\}$, $C=\{2\}$ we have $\tau(A,C)=1$ but $\tau(A,B)+\tau(B,C)=0$.

The trouble is that an infimum only notices the closest pair of points. Let us try a supremum instead:
$$\sigma(E,F) = \sup_{\vv e\in E}d(\vv e,F).$$
This is non-negative, and $\sigma(E,F)=0$ if and only if $d(\vv e,F)=0$ for every $\vv e\in E$, that is (as $F$ is closed) if and only if $E \subseteq F$. So $\sigma$ measures `how far $E$ sticks out of $F$', and is emphatically not symmetric. But it does obey the triangle inequality.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sigma-triangle}
	For non-empty compact $E,F,G$ we have $\sigma(E,G)\leq\sigma(E,F)+\sigma(F,G)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Let $\vv e\in E$. By Lemma \ref{lem:nearest-point} choose $\vv f\in F$ with $\norm{\vv e -\vv f} = d(\vv e,F)$. For any $\vv g\in G$,
	$$d(\vv e,G) \leq \norm{\vv e-\vv g}\leq\norm{\vv e-\vv f}+\norm{\vv f-\vv g} = d(\vv e,F)+\norm{\vv f-\vv g}.$$
	Taking the infimum over $\vv g\in G$ gives $d(\vv e,G)\leq d(\vv e,F)+d(\vv f,G) \leq \sigma(E,F)+\sigma(F,G)$, and taking the supremum over $\vv e \in E$ finishes the proof.
\end{proof}

Symmetrising now gives what we want.

\begin{definition}[Hausdorff Metric]
	Let $\KK$ be the collection of non-empty compact subsets of $\R^m$. For $E,F\in\KK$ set
	$$\rho(E,F) = \sigma(E,F)+\sigma(F,E) = \sup_{\vv e\in E}\inf_{\vv f\in F}\norm{\vv e - \vv f} + \sup_{\vv f\in F}\inf_{\vv e\in E}\norm{\vv e-\vv f}.$$
	We call $\rho$ the \vocab{Hausdorff metric}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
	$\rho$ is a metric on $\KK$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Clearly $\rho\geq0$ and $\rho$ is symmetric. If $\rho(E,F)=0$ then $\sigma(E,F)=\sigma(F,E)=0$, so $E\subseteq F$ and $F\subseteq E$, giving $E=F$; and conversely. The triangle inequality follows from Lemma \ref{lem:sigma-triangle} applied twice:
	$$\rho(E,G) = \sigma(E,G)+\sigma(G,E) \leq \big(\sigma(E,F)+\sigma(F,G)\big)+\big(\sigma(G,F)+\sigma(F,E)\big) = \rho(E,F)+\rho(F,G). \qedhere$$
\end{proof}

What makes $\rho$ genuinely useful is that it is complete. We need two preliminary observations.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nested-compacts}
	Suppose $K_1\supseteq K_2\supseteq K_3\supseteq\cdots$ are non-empty compact subsets of $\R^m$ and let $K = \bigcap_{p\geq1}K_p$. Then $K$ is non-empty and compact, and $\rho(K_p,K)\to0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	$K$ is closed (an intersection of closed sets) and bounded, hence compact. To see it is non-empty, pick $\vv k_p\in K_p$ for each $p$. All the $\vv k_p$ lie in the compact set $K_1$, so some subsequence $\vv k_{p(j)}\to\vv k$. For each fixed $q$ we have $\vv k_{p(j)}\in K_q$ for all $j$ with $p(j)\geq q$, and $K_q$ is closed, so $\vv k\in K_q$. Hence $\vv k\in K$.

	Since $K\subseteq K_p$ we have $\sigma(K,K_p)=0$, so $\rho(K_p,K) = \sigma(K_p,K)$. Suppose this does not tend to $0$. Then there is an $\varepsilon>0$, a subsequence $p(j)$, and points $\vv x_{p(j)}\in K_{p(j)}$ with $d(\vv x_{p(j)},K)\geq\varepsilon$. Passing to a further subsequence, $\vv x_{p(j)}\to\vv x$, and exactly as above $\vv x\in K$. But $d(\cdot,K)$ is continuous, so $d(\vv x,K)\geq\varepsilon>0$, contradicting $\vv x\in K$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:K-plus-ball}
	If $K \subseteq\R^m$ is compact and $r \geq 0$, then $K+\overline{B}(\vv 0,r) = \{\vv x+\vv y : \vv x\in K, \norm{\vv y}\leq r\}$ is compact.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	It is clearly bounded. If $\vv x_n + \vv y_n\to\vv c$ with $\vv x_n\in K$ and $\norm{\vv y_n}\leq r$, pass to a subsequence with $\vv x_{n(j)}\to\vv x\in K$; then $\vv y_{n(j)}\to\vv c-\vv x$, so $\norm{\vv c-\vv x}\leq r$ and $\vv c\in K+\overline{B}(\vv0,r)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hausdorff-complete}
	The Hausdorff metric $\rho$ is complete on $\KK$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	By Lemma \ref{lem:cauchy-tricks}(ii) it suffices to show that any sequence $(E_n)$ in $\KK$ with $\rho(E_n,E_{n+1})\leq 100^{-n}$ converges. Set
	$$K_n = E_n + \overline{B}(\vv0, 10\cdot 100^{-n}),$$
	which is compact by Lemma \ref{lem:K-plus-ball}. Since $\sigma(E_{n+1},E_n)\leq\rho(E_n,E_{n+1})\leq100^{-n}$, every point of $E_{n+1}$ lies within $100^{-n}$ of $E_n$, that is $E_{n+1}\subseteq E_n + \overline B(\vv0,100^{-n})$. Hence
	$$K_{n+1} \subseteq E_n + \overline B(\vv 0, 100^{-n}) + \overline B(\vv0, 10\cdot 100^{-n-1}) \subseteq E_n + \overline B(\vv0, 2\cdot 100^{-n}) \subseteq K_n.$$
	By Lemma \ref{lem:nested-compacts} the set $K = \bigcap_n K_n$ is a non-empty compact set with $\rho(K_n,K)\to0$. Finally $E_n\subseteq K_n$ and every point of $K_n$ is within $10\cdot100^{-n}$ of $E_n$, so $\rho(E_n,K_n)\leq10\cdot100^{-n}\to0$, and therefore
	$$\rho(E_n,K)\leq\rho(E_n,K_n)+\rho(K_n,K)\to0. \qedhere$$
\end{proof}

\section{Runge's Theorem}

Weierstrass's theorem tells us that every continuous real function on $[a,b]$ is a uniform limit of polynomials. Is there a complex analogue? Cauchy's theorem answers with a resounding no.

\begin{example}\label{ex:zbar}
	Let $\dbar = \{z : |z|\leq1\}$ and $f(z) = \overline z$. Then $\sup_{z\in\dbar}|f(z)-P(z)|\geq1$ for every polynomial $P$.

	Indeed, let $C$ be the contour $\theta\mapsto e^{i\theta}$, $0\leq\theta\leq2\pi$. By Cauchy's theorem $\int_C P(z)\dd z = 0$, so
	$$2\pi\norm{f-P}_\infty \geq \left|\int_C\big(\overline z - P(z)\big)\dd z\right| = \left|\int_C\overline z\dd z\right| = \left|\int_0^{2\pi}e^{-i\theta}\cdot ie^{i\theta}\dd\theta\right| = 2\pi. \qedhere$$
\end{example}

That example is unfair: $\overline z$ is nowhere analytic, and we know that a uniform limit of analytic functions is analytic. So let us ask for analytic $f$. Cauchy's theorem still causes trouble.

\begin{example}\label{ex:oneoverz}
	Let $T = \{z : \tfrac12\leq|z|\leq2\}$ and $f(z) = 1/z$, which is analytic on the open set $\Omega = \{z : 0<|z|<3\}\supseteq T$. Then $\sup_{z\in T}|f(z)-P(z)|\geq1$ for every polynomial $P$, since with $C$ as above,
	$$2\pi\norm{f-P}_\infty\geq\left|\int_C\left(\frac1z - P(z)\right)\dd z\right| = |2\pi i| = 2\pi. \qedhere$$
\end{example}

The obstruction here is that $T$ has a hole, and $f$ misbehaves inside it. So the best we can hope for is a theorem about sets without holes, and the following turns out to be the right notion.

\begin{definition}[Path-Connectedness]
	A set $U\subseteq\C$ is \vocab{path-connected} if, given $z_0,z_1\in U$, we can find a continuous $\gamma : [0,1]\to U$ with $\gamma(0)=z_0$ and $\gamma(1)=z_1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Runge]\label{thm:runge}
	Let $\Omega\subseteq\C$ be open and $f : \Omega\to\C$ analytic. Suppose $K\subseteq\Omega$ is compact with $\C\setminus K$ path-connected. Then given $\varepsilon>0$ there is a polynomial $P$ with
	$$\sup_{z\in K}|f(z)-P(z)|<\varepsilon.$$
\end{theorem}

Note that this is genuinely stronger than Taylor's theorem, which says only that $f$ is uniformly approximable by polynomials on any \emph{disc} contained in $\Omega$; Runge's theorem is a global statement. (And there is no hope of simply expanding in a Taylor series: on $\Omega = \{z : 10^{-2}<|z|<1\}\setminus(-\infty,0]$ the function $1/z$ is analytic and bounded but is not given by any single power series.)

The proof falls into three steps. First we express $f$ on $K$ as a sum of contour integrals along segments lying outside $K$; then we approximate each such integral by a finite sum of simple poles; then we show that each simple pole can be pushed off to infinity and replaced by a polynomial. The last step is where path-connectedness enters.

\subsection{Step One: A Contour Representation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:runge-contours}
	Let $\Omega\subseteq\C$ be open and $K\subseteq\Omega$ compact and non-empty. Then we can find finitely many line segments $l_1,\ldots,l_M$, each contained in $\Omega\setminus K$, such that
	$$f(z) = \sum_{r=1}^M\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{l_r}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w$$
	for every $z\in K$ and every analytic $f : \Omega\to\C$. Moreover $d(K, l_1\cup\cdots\cup l_M) > 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Since $K$ is compact and $\C\setminus\Omega$ is closed and disjoint from $K$, Lemma \ref{lem:two-sets-min}(ii) gives a $\delta>0$ with $|k-u|\geq 100\delta$ for all $k \in K$ and $u\notin\Omega$ (if $\Omega=\C$, take any $\delta>0$).

	Tile the plane by the closed squares of side $\delta$ with corners in $\delta\Z + i\delta\Z$, and let $\Gamma$ be the (finite) collection of those squares which meet $K$. Every $S \in \Gamma$ satisfies $S\subseteq K+\overline B(0,2\delta)\subseteq\Omega$, so $f$ is analytic on a neighbourhood of each $S\in\Gamma$. Write $G = \bigcup_{S\in\Gamma}S$. If $z\in K$ then every square containing $z$ meets $K$ and so lies in $\Gamma$; as the squares containing $z$ cover a neighbourhood of $z$, we get $K \subseteq \Int G$.

	\begin{center}
		\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.62]
			\draw[very thick,color=examplefg,smooth] plot coordinates
				{(1.4,1.4) (3.2,0.7) (5.3,1.5) (6.2,3.3) (4.8,4.7) (2.6,4.4) (1.1,3.1) (1.4,1.4)};
			\node[color=examplefg] at (3.6,2.7) {\small $K$};
			\foreach \x in {0,1,...,8} {\draw[thin,gray] (\x,0) -- (\x,6);}
			\foreach \y in {0,1,...,6} {\draw[thin,gray] (0,\y) -- (8,\y);}
			\draw[very thick,color=blue] (1,0) -- (6,0) -- (6,1) -- (7,1) -- (7,4) -- (6,4) -- (6,5)
				-- (4,5) -- (4,4) -- (2,4) -- (2,5) -- (1,5) -- (1,3) -- (0,3) -- (0,1) -- (1,1) -- cycle;
			\node[color=blue] at (7.6,2.5) {\small $l_r$};
		\end{tikzpicture}
	\end{center}

	Now fix $z\in\Int G$ which does not lie on the boundary of any square of the tiling. Then $z$ lies in the interior of exactly one square $S_0$, and $S_0\in\Gamma$ since $z \in G$. Orienting each $\partial S$ anticlockwise, Cauchy's integral formula and Cauchy's theorem give
	$$\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial S}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w = \begin{cases}f(z) & S = S_0,\\ 0 & S\in\Gamma,\ S\neq S_0,\end{cases}$$
	so summing over $S\in\Gamma$ yields $f(z) = \sum_{S\in\Gamma}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial S}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w$.

	Each edge of the tiling which is shared by two squares of $\Gamma$ is traversed once in each direction, so those contributions cancel. Let $l_1,\ldots,l_M$ be the remaining edges -- the edges of squares of $\Gamma$ whose neighbouring square is not in $\Gamma$ -- with their induced orientations. Then
	$$f(z) = \sum_{r=1}^M\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{l_r}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w$$
	for all such $z$. Each $l_r$ lies in $\Omega$, and $l_r\cap K = \emptyset$: if $l_r$ met $K$ then the square on its far side would meet $K$ too and would belong to $\Gamma$, so $l_r$ would have cancelled. Since $\bigcup_r l_r$ and $K$ are disjoint compact sets, Lemma \ref{lem:two-sets-min}(i) gives $d(K,\bigcup_r l_r)>0$.

	Finally, both sides of the displayed identity are continuous functions of $z$ on the open set $\Int G$ (for the right-hand side, because the integrands have denominators bounded away from zero there). They agree on $\Int G$ minus the union of the grid lines, which is dense in the open set $\Int G$. Hence they agree on all of $\Int G \supseteq K$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Step Two: Approximation by Simple Poles}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:runge-poles}
	Let $\Omega$ be open, $K\subseteq\Omega$ compact, and $f:\Omega\to\C$ analytic. Given $\varepsilon>0$ we can find $N$, complex numbers $A_1,\ldots,A_N$ and points $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_N\in\Omega\setminus K$ such that
	$$\left|f(z)-\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{A_n}{z-\alpha_n}\right|\leq\varepsilon$$
	for all $z\in K$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	By Lemma \ref{lem:runge-contours} it suffices to approximate a single term $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_l\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w$, where $l$ is a segment disjoint from $K$ and contained in $\Omega$. Parametrise $l$ by $\gamma(t) = w_0+t(w_1-w_0)$ for $t\in[0,1]$, so that
	$$\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_l\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w = \int_0^1 F(t,z)\dd t, \qquad F(t,z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\cdot\frac{f(\gamma(t))(w_1-w_0)}{\gamma(t)-z}.$$
	Since $d(K,l)>0$, the function $F$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,1]\times K$, hence uniformly continuous there by Theorem \ref{thm:unif-cts}. So given $\eta>0$ there is an $m$ with $|F(t,z)-F(r/m,z)|<\eta$ whenever $t\in[r/m,(r+1)/m]$ and $z\in K$. Therefore
	$$\left|\int_0^1F(t,z)\dd t - \frac1m\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}F(r/m,z)\right| \leq \eta$$
	for all $z\in K$, and the Riemann sum is exactly of the form $\sum_r \frac{A_r}{z-\alpha_r}$ with $\alpha_r = \gamma(r/m) \in l\subseteq\Omega\setminus K$ and suitable constants $A_r$. Doing this for each of the $M$ segments with $\eta = \varepsilon/M$ completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Step Three: Pushing Poles to Infinity}

Everything now reduces to the following statement, which is where the topology of $K$ finally matters.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:runge-pole-poly}
	Let $K\subseteq\C$ be compact with $\C\setminus K$ path-connected. Then for every $\alpha\notin K$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a polynomial $P$ with $\left|P(z)-\frac{1}{z-\alpha}\right|<\varepsilon$ for all $z\in K$.
\end{lemma}

Write $\Lambda(K)$ for the set of $\alpha \notin K$ with this property. We must show $\Lambda(K) = \C\setminus K$, and we do it in three moves.

\begin{proof}
	\textbf{(a) $\Lambda(K)$ contains all sufficiently large $\alpha$.} Choose $R$ with $K\subseteq B(0,R/2)$. If $|\alpha|\geq R$ then for $z \in K$,
	$$\frac{1}{z-\alpha} = \frac{-1}{\alpha(1-z/\alpha)} = -\frac1\alpha\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac z\alpha\right)^n,$$
	and since $|z/\alpha|\leq\frac12$ on $K$ the series converges uniformly on $K$ by the Weierstrass $M$-test. Its partial sums are polynomials, so $\alpha\in\Lambda(K)$.

	\textbf{(b) $\Lambda(K)$ is `locally closed under small moves'.} Suppose $\alpha\in\Lambda(K)$ and $|\alpha-\beta|<\tfrac12 d(\alpha,K)$. Put $\delta = d(\alpha,K)$, so $|z-\alpha|\geq\delta$ for $z\in K$. Then for $z \in K$,
	$$\frac{1}{z-\beta} = \frac{1}{(z-\alpha)-(\beta-\alpha)} = \frac{1}{z-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{z-\alpha}} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(\beta-\alpha)^n}{(z-\alpha)^{n+1}},$$
	and since $\left|\frac{\beta-\alpha}{z-\alpha}\right|\leq\frac12$ on $K$ the series converges uniformly on $K$. So given $\varepsilon>0$ we can find $N$ with
	$$\left|\frac{1}{z-\beta}-\sum_{n=0}^N\frac{(\beta-\alpha)^n}{(z-\alpha)^{n+1}}\right| < \frac\varepsilon2 \qquad (z \in K).$$
	Now $\alpha\in\Lambda(K)$ means $(z-\alpha)^{-1}$ is uniformly approximable on $K$ by polynomials; hence so is each power $(z-\alpha)^{-(n+1)}$ (a product of uniformly bounded uniformly approximable functions is uniformly approximable), and hence so is the finite sum. Choosing a polynomial within $\varepsilon/2$ of it gives $\beta\in\Lambda(K)$.

	\textbf{(c) Path-connectedness.} Let $\beta\notin K$. By (a) choose $\alpha_0$ with $|\alpha_0|$ large enough that $\alpha_0 \in \Lambda(K)$. Since $\C\setminus K$ is path-connected there is a continuous $\gamma:[0,1]\to\C\setminus K$ with $\gamma(0)=\alpha_0$, $\gamma(1)=\beta$. The image $\gamma([0,1])$ is compact and disjoint from the compact set $K$, so by Lemma \ref{lem:two-sets-min}(i) there is a $\delta>0$ with $d(\gamma(t),K)\geq 4\delta$ for all $t$. Also $\gamma$ is uniformly continuous, so there is an $N$ with $|\gamma(s)-\gamma(t)|<\delta$ whenever $|s-t|\leq 1/N$. Setting $w_j = \gamma(j/N)$ we have $|w_j - w_{j+1}| < \delta \leq \tfrac12 d(w_j,K)$, so by (b) and induction $w_0\in\Lambda(K)$ gives $w_N = \beta\in\Lambda(K)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Runge's theorem]
	Given $\varepsilon>0$, Lemma \ref{lem:runge-poles} provides $A_n$ and $\alpha_n\notin K$ with $\left|f(z)-\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{A_n}{z-\alpha_n}\right|\leq\varepsilon/2$ on $K$. By Lemma \ref{lem:runge-pole-poly} each $\frac{1}{z-\alpha_n}$ is within $\frac{\varepsilon}{2N(1+|A_n|)}$ of a polynomial $P_n$ uniformly on $K$. Then $P = \sum_n A_nP_n$ satisfies $\sup_K|f-P|<\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An Application: Pointwise Limits Need Not Be Continuous}

If analytic functions converge \emph{uniformly}, the limit is analytic. It is natural to ask whether uniformity is really needed, and Runge's theorem lets us answer emphatically.

\begin{example}\label{ex:runge-pointwise}
	Let $D = \{z:|z|<1\}$ and define $f : \C\to\C$ by $f(0)=0$ and
	$$f(re^{i\theta}) = r^{3/2}e^{3i\theta/2}\qquad (r>0,\ 0<\theta\leq2\pi).$$
	Then $f$ is not continuous on $D$ (approaching a positive real $x$ from above gives $x^{3/2}$, from below gives $-x^{3/2}$), yet there are polynomials $P_n$ with $P_n(z)\to f(z)$ for every $z\in D$.
\end{example}

\begin{proof}
	The function $f$ is analytic on $\Omega = \C\setminus[0,\infty)$, being a branch of $z^{3/2}$ there. For $n\geq1$ let $f_n(z) = f(z-i/n)$, which is analytic on $\Omega_n = \C\setminus\left(\tfrac in + [0,\infty)\right)$, and let
	$$K_n = \left\{z : |z|\leq 1,\ d\left(z,\tfrac in+[0,\infty)\right)\geq \tfrac{1}{n^2}\right\}.$$
	Then $K_n$ is compact, $K_n\subseteq\Omega_n$, and $\C\setminus K_n$ is path-connected: $K_n$ is a closed disc with a slit-shaped neighbourhood removed, and any point of the complement can be joined to infinity by going along the slit and out. By Runge's theorem there is a polynomial $P_n$ with $\sup_{z\in K_n}|f_n(z)-P_n(z)|\leq2^{-n}$.

	Now fix $z$ with $|z|<1$. If $z$ is a non-negative real then $d(z,\tfrac in+[0,\infty)) = \tfrac1n\geq\tfrac1{n^2}$, so $z\in K_n$ for every $n$. Otherwise $d(z,[0,\infty)) = c > 0$ and so $d(z,\tfrac in +[0,\infty))\geq c - \tfrac1n \geq \tfrac{1}{n^2}$ for all large $n$. Either way $z\in K_n$ eventually, and then
	$$|P_n(z)-f(z)| \leq 2^{-n} + |f(z - i/n)-f(z)| \to 0,$$
	since $z-i/n\to z$ from below, and $f$ \emph{is} continuous from below across the positive real axis (as $\theta\to2\pi^-$ we get $r^{3/2}e^{3i\pi} = -r^{3/2}$, which is the assigned value at $\theta = 2\pi$); at $z=0$ we simply have $f(-i/n) = n^{-3/2}e^{9i\pi/4}\to0=f(0)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Odd Numbers}

According to Von Neumann, `in mathematics you don't understand things, you just get used to them'. The real line is a case in point. A single innocuous-looking axiom -- every bounded increasing sequence converges -- calls into being an object of indescribable complexity. We know that $\R$ is uncountable; on the other hand, if our alphabet has $n$ symbols then there are at most $n^m$ phrases of length exactly $m$, so the collection of \emph{describable} real numbers is a countable union of finite sets, and hence countable. Almost every real number cannot be described at all.

In this section we look at a few of the prettier shells: numbers we can describe, and about which we can say something.

\subsection{Irrationality}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:e-irrational}
	The number $e$ is irrational.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Suppose $e = p/q$ with $p,q\in\N$. Recall $e = \sum_{r=0}^\infty\frac{1}{r!}$. Consider
	$$N = q!\left(e - \sum_{r=0}^q\frac{1}{r!}\right) = q!\,e - \sum_{r=0}^q\frac{q!}{r!}.$$
	Now $q!\,e = q!\,p/q = (q-1)!\,p$ is an integer, and each $q!/r!$ with $r\leq q$ is an integer, so $N \in\Z$. On the other hand
	$$0 < N = q!\sum_{r=q+1}^\infty\frac{1}{r!} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{q!}{(q+k)!} \leq \sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(q+1)^k} = \frac{1}{q}\leq 1,$$
	and the inequality $q!/(q+k)! \leq (q+1)^{-k}$ is strict for $k\geq2$, so $0<N<1$. There is no such integer.
\end{proof}

The proof of the corresponding statement for $\pi$ is harder, and rests on the following computation.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pi-integral}
	Write $f_n(x) = x^n(\pi-x)^n$. Then
	$$\int_0^\pi f_n(x)\sin x\dd x = n!\sum_{j=0}^n a_j\pi^j$$
	for some integers $a_j$ (depending on $n$).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Expanding, $f_n(x) = \sum_{r=0}^n\binom{n}{r}(-1)^r\pi^{n-r}x^{n+r}$, a polynomial with terms of degrees between $n$ and $2n$. So $f_n^{(k)}(0) = 0$ for $k<n$ and for $k>2n$, while for $n\leq k\leq 2n$ only the term with $n+r = k$ survives and
	$$f_n^{(k)}(0) = k!\binom{n}{k-n}(-1)^{k-n}\pi^{2n-k}.$$
	Since $n\leq k$, the number $k!/n!$ is an integer, so $f_n^{(k)}(0) = n!\,m_k\,\pi^{2n-k}$ for some $m_k \in \Z$, and the exponent $2n-k$ lies between $0$ and $n$. Moreover $f_n(\pi-x) = f_n(x)$, so $f_n^{(k)}(\pi) = (-1)^kf_n^{(k)}(0)$ is of the same shape.

	Now integrate by parts twice. Writing $I_k = \int_0^\pi f_n^{(k)}(x)\sin x\dd x$,
	\begin{align*}
		I_k &= \Big[-f_n^{(k)}(x)\cos x\Big]_0^\pi + \int_0^\pi f_n^{(k+1)}(x)\cos x \dd x = f_n^{(k)}(\pi)+f_n^{(k)}(0) + \int_0^\pi f_n^{(k+1)}\cos x\dd x,
	\end{align*}
	and $\int_0^\pi f_n^{(k+1)}\cos x\dd x = \left[f_n^{(k+1)}\sin x\right]_0^\pi - I_{k+2} = -I_{k+2}$. Hence
	$$I_k = f_n^{(k)}(\pi)+f_n^{(k)}(0) - I_{k+2}.$$
	Since $f_n^{(k)}\equiv0$ for $k>2n$, we have $I_k = 0$ for $k>2n$, and iterating the recursion downwards from $k=0$ expresses $I_0 = \int_0^\pi f_n\sin x\dd x$ as an integer combination of the numbers $f_n^{(k)}(0)$ and $f_n^{(k)}(\pi)$. Each of these is $n!$ times an integer times a power $\pi^j$ with $0\leq j\leq n$, which is the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:pi-irrational}
	The number $\pi$ is irrational.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Write $P_n(X) = \sum_{j=0}^na_jX^j\in\Z[X]$ as in Lemma \ref{lem:pi-integral}, so that $\int_0^\pi f_n(x)\sin x\dd x = n!\,P_n(\pi)$. On $(0,\pi)$ we have $x^n(\pi-x)^n\sin x > 0$, so this integral is strictly positive. On the other hand, by AM--GM, $x(\pi-x)\leq(\pi/2)^2$, so
	$$0 < n!\,P_n(\pi) = \int_0^\pi f_n(x)\sin x\dd x \leq \int_0^\pi\left(\frac\pi2\right)^{2n}\dd x = \pi\left(\frac{\pi^2}{4}\right)^{n}.$$

	Suppose now $\pi = p/q$ with $p,q\in\N$. Since $\deg P_n\leq n$, the number $q^nP_n(p/q)$ is an integer, and it is strictly positive, so $q^nP_n(\pi)\geq1$. Hence
	$$n! \leq n!\,q^nP_n(\pi) \leq \pi\left(\frac{q\pi^2}{4}\right)^n.$$
	But $C^n/n!\to0$ for every constant $C$, so this fails for large $n$.
\end{proof}

Faced with a proof like that, the reader may cry `how did you think of looking at $f_n$?'. The honest answer is `I did not, I learnt it from somebody else'; but even granting that we could not have invented it in a thousand years, once presented with it we can see a path which might have led there. We are all familiar with the fact that $\int_0^\pi x^n\sin x\dd x$ takes the form $U(\pi)$ for a polynomial $U$ of degree at most $n$ with integer coefficients, and hence so does $\int_0^\pi Q(x)\sin x\dd x$ for any $Q\in\Z[X]$. If $\pi=p/q$ then $q^nU(\pi)$ is an integer. So we experiment, trying to squeeze $\int_0^\pi Q(x)\sin x\dd x$ between $0$ and $1$ in the manner of our proof for $e$; the factor $n!$ appears because we need the integral to be very small, and the symmetric choice $x^n(\pi-x)^n$ is the natural one.

It is worth remembering that after three hundred years we still do not know whether Euler's constant
$$\gamma = \lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n - \log N\right)$$
is irrational.

\subsection{Transcendence}

If the rationals are the best understood numbers, the algebraic numbers are the next best understood.

\begin{definition}[Algebraic and Transcendental]
	A real number $\alpha$ is \vocab{algebraic} if it is a root of some non-zero polynomial with integer coefficients. Otherwise $\alpha$ is \vocab{transcendental}.
\end{definition}

Multiplying up denominators, $\alpha$ is algebraic if and only if it is a root of a non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients.

\begin{lemma}
	The set of algebraic numbers is countable. Consequently transcendental numbers exist, and indeed the transcendental numbers are uncountable.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	For each $n$ and each bound $B$ there are finitely many integer polynomials of degree at most $n$ with all coefficients at most $B$ in modulus, and each has at most $n$ roots. So the algebraic numbers are a countable union of finite sets, hence countable. Since $\R$ is uncountable, the complement is uncountable.
\end{proof}

This argument is Cantor's, and it is beautiful but entirely non-constructive: it tells us that transcendentals abound while showing us none. Historically, the first proof came earlier, and is due to Liouville. It is longer, but it produces actual examples. The key point is that algebraic numbers are \emph{badly approximable} by rationals.

\begin{theorem}[Liouville]\label{thm:liouville}
	Let $\alpha$ be an irrational root of a polynomial $P(X) = a_nX^n+\cdots+a_0$ with $a_j\in\Z$, $n\geq1$ and $a_n\neq0$. Then there is a constant $c = c(\alpha)>0$ such that
	$$\left|\alpha-\frac pq\right| \geq \frac{c}{q^n}$$
	for all $p,q\in\Z$ with $q\geq1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	Since $P$ has finitely many roots, we can find $\delta>0$ such that $P$ has no root other than $\alpha$ in $J = [\alpha-\delta,\alpha+\delta]$. As $P'$ is continuous on the compact set $J$, it is bounded there, say $|P'|\leq M$ with $M > 0$; by the mean value theorem $|P(t)-P(s)|\leq M|t-s|$ for $s,t\in J$.

	Let $p,q\in\Z$ with $q\geq1$. Suppose first that $p/q \in J$. Then $P(p/q)\neq0$, since $\alpha$ is the only root of $P$ in $J$ and $\alpha$ is irrational. But $q^nP(p/q) = \sum_j a_jp^jq^{n-j}$ is an integer, so $|q^nP(p/q)|\geq1$ and hence $|P(p/q)|\geq q^{-n}$. Therefore
	$$\left|\alpha - \frac pq\right| \geq \frac{1}{M}\left|P(\alpha)-P(p/q)\right| = \frac{|P(p/q)|}{M} \geq \frac{1}{Mq^n}.$$
	If instead $p/q\notin J$ then $\left|\alpha - \frac pq\right| > \delta \geq \delta/q^n$. So $c = \min\{1/M,\delta\}$ works.
\end{proof}

The content of the theorem is best seen geometrically. Rationals with denominator $q$ are spaced $1/q$ apart, so we should expect to be able to find one within $1/(2q)$ of $\alpha$, and the theory of continued fractions in the next section will do much better, giving one within $1/q^2$. Liouville's theorem says that for an algebraic $\alpha$ of degree $n$ we can never do better than $c/q^n$: around each algebraic number there is a forbidden zone which the good rational approximations may not enter.

Turning this on its head: if a number \emph{can} be approximated absurdly well by rationals, it cannot be algebraic.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:liouville-number}
	The number $\displaystyle\gamma = \sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{1}{10^{j!}} = 1.110001000000000000000001\ldots$ is transcendental.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	First, $\gamma$ is irrational: its decimal expansion consists of $1$'s in the places $j!$ and $0$'s elsewhere, and so is neither terminating nor eventually periodic (the gaps between consecutive $1$'s grow without bound).

	Let $N\geq1$ and put $\frac{p_N}{q_N} = \sum_{j=0}^N10^{-j!}$, where $q_N = 10^{N!}$ and $p_N\in\N$. Then
	$$0 < \gamma - \frac{p_N}{q_N} = \sum_{j=N+1}^\infty 10^{-j!} \leq \sum_{k=(N+1)!}^\infty 10^{-k} = \frac{10}{9}\cdot 10^{-(N+1)!} \leq \frac{2}{q_N^{N+1}},$$
	using $(N+1)! = (N+1)\cdot N!$, so that $10^{-(N+1)!} = q_N^{-(N+1)}$.

	Suppose $\gamma$ were algebraic, of degree $n$ say, and let $c>0$ be as in Theorem \ref{thm:liouville}. Then for every $N$,
	$$\frac{c}{q_N^n}\leq\left|\gamma-\frac{p_N}{q_N}\right| \leq \frac{2}{q_N^{N+1}}, \quad\text{so}\quad c \leq \frac{2}{q_N^{N+1-n}}.$$
	Taking $N\geq n+1$ and letting $N\to\infty$, the right-hand side tends to $0$ (as $q_N\to\infty$), contradicting $c>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	Replacing $\gamma$ by $\sum_{j\geq0}\varepsilon_j10^{-j!}$ with $\varepsilon_j\in\{1,2\}$ gives an uncountable family of such numbers, all transcendental by the same argument. So Liouville's method also proves that there are uncountably many transcendentals, and this time we can point at them.
\end{remark}

Numbers which admit approximations of this quality are called \vocab{Liouville numbers}. They are, in a precise sense, very rare -- the set of them has Lebesgue measure zero -- yet they are the only transcendentals we can construct with bare hands. As one might expect, proving that a \emph{naturally occurring} number is transcendental is much harder. Hermite proved that $e$ is transcendental, and Lindemann adapted his method to show that $\pi$ is transcendental, thereby settling the two-thousand-year-old problem of squaring the circle in the negative. These proofs are not part of this course; Alan Baker's \emph{Transcendental Number Theory} contains accessible (but miraculous) versions of both.
